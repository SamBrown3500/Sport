1966–1975: Franchise founding and early development
The Chicago Bulls entered the National Basketball Association as an expansion franchise on
January 16, 1966, when the league awarded Chicago its third NBA team after the defunct
Stags and Packers/Zephyrs. The franchise was established by Dick Klein, a former professional
basketball player with the Chicago American Gears who became the team's founding owner
and general manager, marking him as the only Bulls owner with prior pro playing experience.
Along with the San Diego Rockets, the Bulls participated in the 1966 NBA expansion draft,
selecting from a pool of players dispersed by existing teams to build their initial roster. Klein's
group paid $1.1 million for the franchise, reflecting the NBA's push to expand amid growing
popularity in the mid-1960s.
To name the team, Klein drew inspiration from Chicago's historic Union Stock Yards,
symbolizing the city's industrial strength and the animal's aggressive, charging nature. The
"Bulls" moniker was chosen directly by Klein without a public contest, aligning with the team's
early home arena, the International Amphitheatre, located adjacent to the stockyards. Johnny
"Red" Kerr, a veteran NBA center and recent Syracuse Nationals player, was hired as the
inaugural head coach, bringing his playing insight to guide the expansion squad. In the
expansion draft and subsequent moves, the Bulls acquired foundational talent, including a
trade for All-Star point guard Guy Rodgers from the San Francisco Warriors on September 7,
1966, in exchange for future players Jim King and Jeff Mullins plus cash considerations—a deal
that underscored the team's immediate need for veteran leadership.
The 1966–67 inaugural season saw the Bulls finish 33–48 under Kerr, a respectable mark for
an expansion team that went 1–0 in its debut but struggled with integration and depth.
Progress came swiftly in 1967–68, when the team posted a 29–53 record yet earned its first
playoff berth as the Western Division's fourth seed, facing the Los Angeles Lakers in the
division semifinals and losing 4–1. This appearance highlighted emerging grit, particularly from
guard Jerry Sloan, selected fourth overall in the expansion draft from the Baltimore Bullets;
Sloan's relentless defense and rebounding—averaging 10.2 rebounds per game as a guard—
helped define the Bulls' tough, blue-collar identity. Forward Bob Love, acquired via trade from
the Cincinnati Royals in 1968, added scoring punch with his mid-range efficiency, while Chet
Walker joined in a 1969 blockbuster trade from the Philadelphia 76ers for two draft picks,
providing All-Star caliber forward play with 20.5 points per game in his debut Bulls season.
These players formed the core of a defensive-oriented squad that emphasized hustle
over flash.
By the early 1970s, the Bulls achieved sustained contention, capturing Midwest Division titles
in 1970–71 (51–31 record, second-best in the Western Conference) and 1971–72 (57–25,

third-best league-wide), both under coach Dick Motta, who succeeded Kerr in 1968. In 1970–
71, powered by Sloan's All-Defensive honors and Walker's scoring, the team advanced to the
Western Conference semifinals before falling to the Lakers in seven games. The 1971–72
campaign featured similar defensive prowess—holding opponents to 102.8 points per game,
third-lowest in the NBA—but ended in a sweep by the Lakers in the semifinals. Love's
emergence as a 25-point-per-game scorer in 1972–73 further solidified the frontcourt
alongside Walker, fostering a team culture of resilience amid limited star power compared to
rivals like the Lakers or Knicks.
Financial pressures plagued the franchise from the outset, exacerbated by low attendance at
the aging Amphitheatre and competition from Chicago's entrenched sports teams, including
the NFL's Bears and MLB's Cubs and White Sox. Cash-strapped operations led to cost-cutting
trades, such as shipping Rodgers to the Cincinnati Royals early in 1967–68 for financial relief,
and culminated in Klein selling majority control to Chicago magnate Arthur Wirtz in 1972 for
$3.3 million amid mounting debts. Relocation threats loomed during this instability, with
discussions of moving to cities like Virginia Beach, though Wirtz's investment stabilized the
team in Chicago. These challenges tempered early optimism but built a foundation of
perseverance, setting the stage for the 1976 ABA-NBA merger that brought center Artis
Gilmore aboard and elevated expectations.

1976–1984: Artis Gilmore and Reggie Theus era
The 1976 ABA-NBA merger provided the Chicago Bulls with a significant boost, as the team
selected center Artis Gilmore in the subsequent dispersal draft from the disbanded Kentucky
Colonels for a record $1.1 million signing fee. Gilmore, a five-time ABA All-Star and
dominant interior force known for his rebounding and shot-blocking prowess, immediately
anchored the Bulls' frontcourt, averaging 19.0 points and 12.7 rebounds per game in his debut
1976-77 season while earning All-NBA Second Team honors. Over his seven seasons with
Chicago from 1976 to 1983, Gilmore earned six NBA All-Star selections (1978, 1979, 19811983, and another in 1985 after a trade), solidifying his role as the team's defensive
cornerstone and leading the league in field goal percentage multiple times, including a 65.3%
mark in 1978-79. His presence helped elevate the Bulls from perennial also-rans to playoff
contenders, culminating in a franchise-record 44 wins during the 1976-77 campaign under
new head coach Ed Badger.
The Bulls further strengthened their offense in 1978 by drafting UNLV guard Reggie Theus
ninth overall in the NBA Draft, pairing him with Gilmore to form a dynamic scoring duo that
emphasized fast-break play and interior dominance. Theus, a 6'7" versatile forward who
could handle the ball and shoot from range, quickly emerged as a high-volume scorer,
averaging 23.8 points per game in his 1979-80 season and earning back-to-back All-Star
nods in 1979 and 1980. Together, Gilmore and Theus combined for over 40 points per game
in several seasons, with Theus setting Bulls single-season scoring records, including 2,379

points in 1982-83, a mark that stood until surpassed in the Michael Jordan era. Their
partnership peaked in the 1980-81 season, where the duo powered a 45-37 record—the
team's best since 1972—under head coach Jerry Sloan, who succeeded Badger after interim
stints by Larry Costello and Scotty Robertson.
Coaching transitions marked the era's volatility, beginning with Dick Motta's departure after
the 1975-76 season following eight years of steady improvement, replaced by Badger, who
guided the team to its first playoff appearance since 1975 in 1976-77 before a first-round
sweep by the Boston Celtics. Subsequent changes included Costello's brief 1978 tenure
and Robertson's partial 1978-79 season, both amid roster adjustments, before Sloan's arrival
in 1979 brought defensive discipline and culminated in the 1980-81 playoff berth, where the
Bulls fell in the first round to the Milwaukee Bucks despite sweeping the season series against
them. Kevin Loughery took over as head coach in 1983-84, inheriting a squad led by Theus
and a traded Gilmore, but the team struggled to a 27-55 record amid injuries and
inconsistency. Key events included the 1981-82 season's 34-48 finish under Sloan,
hampered by Gilmore's knee issues, yet featuring emerging rivalries within the newly formed
Central Division, such as heated matchups with the Detroit Pistons, who finished second that
year and foreshadowed future Central Division battles.
Off the court, the Bulls operated under the ownership of the Wirtz family, led by Arthur Wirtz,
who acquired controlling interest in 1972 from founder Dick Klein and maintained stewardship
through the era, prioritizing fiscal stability over aggressive spending. Attendance at Chicago
Stadium improved modestly with Gilmore's arrival, rising from an average of 9,244 fans per
game in 1975-76 to 10,365 in 1976-77, reflecting renewed interest in the contending team,
though it fluctuated between 7,000 and 9,000 in subsequent years amid inconsistent
results. The era of contention driven by Gilmore and Theus concluded with the 1984
NBA Draft, where the Bulls selected Michael Jordan third overall, signaling the dawn of a
transformative chapter.

1984–1993: Arrival of Michael Jordan and first three-peat
The Chicago Bulls selected Michael Jordan third overall in the 1984 NBA Draft, a pivotal
moment that began the franchise's ascent to prominence. As a rookie in the 1984–85
season, Jordan averaged 28.2 points per game, earning NBA Rookie of the Year honors and
leading the Bulls to a 38–44 record, a 11-win improvement from the prior year. Despite his
individual brilliance, the team was swept 3–0 by the Milwaukee Bucks in the first round of the
playoffs, highlighting the need for complementary talent around their star.
To build a contending roster, the Bulls focused on acquiring versatile players to support Jordan.
In the 1987 NBA Draft, they selected Scottie Pippen fifth overall from the University of Central
Arkansas after orchestrating a trade with the Seattle SuperSonics, and Horace Grant tenth
overall from Clemson University, forming the core of a dynamic frontcourt. These additions

bolstered the team's defense and spacing, with Pippen emerging as an elite perimeter
defender and Grant providing rebounding and interior presence. By the late 1980s, Jordan's
scoring prowess—coupled with these reinforcements—elevated the Bulls from perennial
underdogs to playoff regulars, though they still faced stiff Eastern Conference competition.
In 1989, the Bulls hired Phil Jackson as head coach, replacing Doug Collins and ushering in a
new strategic era. Jackson, influenced by assistant coach Tex Winter, implemented the
triangle offense, a fluid system emphasizing ball movement, spacing, and read-and-react
principles to maximize team efficiency rather than isolating Jordan. This tactical shift paid
dividends immediately; in the 1987–88 season under Collins, the Bulls had already achieved a
franchise-record 50–32 mark and reached the Eastern Conference Semifinals, but Jackson's
arrival refined their execution.
The culmination of this transformation arrived in the 1990–91 season, when the Bulls posted a
league-best 61–21 record and advanced to the NBA Finals against the Los Angeles Lakers.
Chicago won the series 4–1, securing their first championship with a 108–101 victory in Game
5, where Jordan's 30 points and Pippen's defensive assignment on Magic Johnson proved
decisive. Jordan was named Finals MVP, averaging 31.2 points, 6.6 rebounds, and 11.4
assists per game. The following year, the Bulls repeated as champions, defeating the Portland
Trail Blazers 4–2 in the 1992 Finals, highlighted by a dramatic 15-point fourth-quarter
comeback in Game 6 led by Jordan's 22 points in the period. In 1993, they completed the
three-peat by edging the Phoenix Suns 4–2, clinching the title with a 99–98 win in Game 6
on John Paxson's three-pointer, despite Jordan's grueling 55-point effort across Games 5 and
6.
During this era, Jordan amassed seven consecutive NBA scoring titles from 1987 to 1993,
along with the 1988 league MVP and Defensive Player of the Year awards, underscoring his
two-way dominance. He averaged 35.0 points per game in 1987–88 while leading the
league in steals with 3.2 per game, a rare feat for a guard. The Bulls' success was also fueled
by intensifying rivalries, particularly with the Detroit Pistons, whose "Jordan Rules" strategy— a
physical, double-team tactic designed to force Jordan left and wear him down—dominated
playoff matchups from 1988 to 1990, pushing Chicago to adapt and ultimately overcome the
"Bad Boys" in 1991. This period of growth transformed the Bulls into a dynasty, though it
ended abruptly with Jordan's retirement in October 1993 following his father's death.

1993–1998: Jordan's retirements, returns, and second three-peat
Following the Chicago Bulls' third consecutive NBA championship in June 1993, Michael
Jordan abruptly retired from basketball on October 6, 1993, citing a loss of desire to play amid
personal tragedy. His father, James Jordan, had been murdered in July 1993 during a
roadside robbery in North Carolina, an event that deeply affected the star guard and prompted
him to pursue a childhood dream of professional baseball. Jordan signed a minor league

contract with the Chicago White Sox organization and played as an outfielder for their DoubleSign in
A affiliate, the Birmingham Barons, during the 1994 season, where he batted .202 with three
home runs in 127 games.
Without Jordan, the Bulls relied heavily on Scottie Pippen to lead the team in the 1993–94
season, finishing with a 55–27 record and securing the second seed in the Eastern
Conference. Pippen emerged as a dominant force, averaging 22.0 points, 8.7 rebounds,
and 5.6 assists per game while earning third place in MVP voting, as Chicago advanced to the
Eastern Conference semifinals before losing to the New York Knicks in seven games.
Croatian forward Toni Kukoč, who had been drafted by the Bulls in 1990 but delayed his arrival
until after Jordan's retirement, made an immediate impact off the bench in his rookie year,
averaging 10.9 points and earning NBA All-Rookie Second Team honors for his versatile
scoring and playmaking.
The 1994–95 season saw further challenges for the Pippen-led Bulls, who posted a 47–35
record and earned the third seed in the Central Division despite a midseason boost from
Jordan's return. Jordan rejoined the team on March 19, 1995, announcing his comeback via
a fax to an NBA reporter that simply read "I'm back," and he played in 17 regular-season games
while wearing number 45. Chicago swept the Charlotte Hornets in the first round but fell to
the Orlando Magic 4–1 in the Eastern Conference semifinals, marking their deepest playoff run
without a Finals appearance since Jordan's departure.
Jordan's full return in the 1995–96 season propelled the Bulls to unprecedented heights, as
they achieved a 72–10 regular-season record, tying the NBA's all-time wins mark at the time
and earning the top seed in the East. Under head coach Phil Jackson's "Zen Master"
philosophy, which emphasized mindfulness, team harmony, and the triangle offense—a fluid,
read-and-react system designed to maximize ball movement and player versatility—the Bulls
dominated the playoffs, sweeping the Seattle SuperSonics 4–0 in the NBA Finals to claim their
fourth championship overall. Jordan averaged 30.4 points in the Finals series, earning his
fourth Finals MVP award.
To bolster their rebounding and defensive intensity for the subsequent title runs, the Bulls
added colorful power forward Dennis Rodman in October 1995 as a free agent from the San
Antonio Spurs, where he had led the league in rebounds the prior season. Veteran point
guard Steve Kerr, who had signed with Chicago in 1993, became a key reserve during this era,
providing sharpshooting and leadership while ranking second in the NBA in three-point
percentage in 1995–96. Jackson's holistic approach, blending psychological preparation
with tactical innovation, fostered a cohesive unit that repeated as champions in 1997,
defeating the Utah Jazz 4–2 in the Finals; Jordan's iconic "Flu Game" in Game 5 saw him score
38 points despite severe illness from food poisoning.

The dynasty culminated in the 1997–98 season, with the Bulls finishing 62–20 and again
overcoming the Jazz in the Finals, this time 4–2. In Game 6 on June 14, 1998, Jordan hit the
series-clinching jumper over Bryon Russell with 5.2 seconds left, securing his sixth
championship and sixth Finals MVP award. Off the court, Jordan's cultural prominence
soared with the release of Space Jam on November 15, 1996, a Warner Bros. film blending liveaction and animation where he teamed with Looney Tunes characters, grossing over $230
million worldwide and becoming the highest-grossing basketball movie ever. Jordan's
second retirement shortly after the 1998 title ushered in a prolonged era of struggles for
the franchise.

1998–2008: Post-Jordan rebuild and struggles
Following Michael Jordan's retirement in January 1999, shortly after the resolution of the 1998
NBA lockout that shortened the prior season to 50 games, the Chicago Bulls entered a
deliberate rebuilding phase under general manager Jerry Krause. Krause orchestrated a
complete roster overhaul, trading Scottie Pippen to the Houston Rockets on January 19, 1999,
for a second-round draft pick, forward Walt Williams, and center Brad Miller, while releasing
Dennis Rodman as a free agent; he later signed with the Los Angeles Lakers. Additional
moves included trading Steve Kerr to the San Antonio Spurs and Luc Longley to the Phoenix
Suns, effectively dismantling the core of the six-time championship team from the 1990s.
Krause's strategy emphasized youth and draft capital, but it drew immediate backlash from
fans, who chanted "Fire Krause" during home games in the 1997–98 season and displayed
critical signs at the United Center amid the organization's pivot.
The ensuing era, often dubbed the "Baby Bulls," spanned from 1998 to 2004 and featured a
young, inexperienced roster plagued by poor performance and injuries. Krause prioritized high
school and college prospects, selecting center Tyson Chandler (second overall in 2001) and
center Eddy Curry (fourth overall in 2001) to anchor the frontcourt with their athleticism and
size, both standing at 7 feet tall. The team added point guard Jay Williams, the second
overall pick in 2002 out of Duke, who showed early promise with averages of 12.0 points and
6.0 assists in 2002–03 before a career-ending motorcycle accident in June 2003 severed
nerves in his leg and damaged blood vessels, sidelining him permanently. These selections
contributed to historically dismal records, including the franchise-worst 13–69 mark in the
lockout-shortened 1998–99 season, followed by 17–65 in 1999–2000, 15–67 in 2000–01, 21–
61 in 2001–02, 30–52 in 2002–03, and 23–59 in 2003–04, marking six consecutive losing
seasons and multiple sub-25-win campaigns that positioned the Bulls among the league's
worst teams. Krause's decisions, such as trading promising rookie Elton Brand (the 1999 No.
1 pick) to the Los Angeles Clippers in 2001 for draft assets that ultimately yielded Chandler
after a failed deal involving Steve Francis, further highlighted the rebuild's risks and fan
frustrations.

From 2004 to 2008, the Bulls continued rebuilding under new general manager John Paxson,
who succeeded Krause in 2003, focusing on a more balanced core while owner Jerry
Reinsdorf exerted influence through cost-conscious decisions that limited aggressive
spending in free agency. Key additions included guard Kirk Hinrich (seventh overall in
2003), who became a defensive staple, and in the 2004 draft, forward Luol Deng (seventh
overall, acquired via trade from the Phoenix Suns) and guard Ben Gordon (third overall),
forming a promising young trio that emphasized perimeter scoring and versatility. Under
coach Scott Skiles, hired in 2003, the team improved to 40–42 in 2004–05—their first
winning record since 1997–98—and earned a playoff berth, though they fell in five games to
the Washington Wizards; similar progress continued with 41–41 in 2005–06 and a first-round
exit to the Miami Heat. Efforts to accelerate the turnaround faltered in 2005 free agency,
when the Bulls pursued but ultimately failed to land high-profile targets like Elton Brand, who
remained with the Clippers on a long-term deal, underscoring Reinsdorf's reluctance to
exceed salary cap thresholds aggressively.
The decade's low points amplified fan discontent, with Reinsdorf's ownership drawing criticism
for prioritizing financial stability over rapid contention, leading to persistent "Fire Krause"
echoes even after his 2003 departure due to health issues. The Bulls endured prolonged
irrelevance, missing the playoffs in five of the ten seasons and compiling an overall 251–471
record (.348 winning percentage) from 1998–99 to 2007–08, a stark contrast to their prior
dynasty. This sustained futility secured high draft lottery odds, setting the stage for the
selection of point guard Derrick Rose first overall in 2008.

2008–2016: Derrick Rose era and Eastern Conference contention
The Chicago Bulls selected point guard Derrick Rose from the University of Memphis with the
first overall pick in the 2008 NBA Draft, marking a pivotal moment in the franchise's rebuilding
efforts. As a Chicago native, Rose quickly assumed a leadership role, averaging 16.8 points
and 6.3 assists per game in his rookie season of 2008–09, leading all first-year players in
assists and earning unanimous NBA Rookie of the Year honors. His explosive athleticism
and playmaking ability transformed the Bulls' backcourt, helping the team improve to 41–41
and secure a playoff berth for the first time since 2007.
The arrival of Tom Thibodeau as head coach in 2010 elevated the Bulls to elite status, with the
team posting a franchise-best 62–20 record in the 2010–11 regular season and claiming the
No. 1 seed in the Eastern Conference. Rose, in his third NBA season, emerged as the
league's youngest MVP at age 22, averaging 25.0 points and 7.7 assists while leading Chicago
to the Eastern Conference Finals, where they fell to the Miami Heat in five games.
Thibodeau's emphasis on gritty, physical defense—rooted in his prior success as an assistant
with the Boston Celtics—instilled a tough identity, as the Bulls ranked first in defensive rating at
100.4 points allowed per 100 possessions. This blend of Rose's offensive brilliance and
Thibodeau's schemes positioned Chicago as a legitimate contender.

Rose's career trajectory shifted dramatically in the 2012 playoffs when he suffered a torn
anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) in his left knee during Game 1 of the first round against the
Philadelphia 76ers on April 28, scoring 23 points before the injury ended his postseason.
The Bulls advanced to the conference semifinals without him but lost to the Heat in six games,
highlighting the team's depth yet underscoring Rose's irreplaceable impact. He missed the
entire 2012–13 season recovering from surgery, and upon returning in October 2013, Rose
played just 10 games before tearing his medial meniscus in his right knee against the Portland
Trail Blazers in November, sidelining him for the remainder of the 2013–14 campaign. These
knee issues limited Rose to only 50 regular-season appearances over the next two years,
testing the Bulls' resilience amid ongoing contention.
Despite the absences, the Bulls maintained competitiveness through key contributors like
center Joakim Noah, who anchored the frontcourt with his rebounding and shot-blocking
prowess. In 2013–14, Noah earned NBA Defensive Player of the Year honors, averaging 12.5
rebounds and 1.5 blocks per game while helping Chicago rank third in defensive rating despite
Rose's limited play. Selected 30th overall in the 2011 NBA Draft, wing Jimmy Butler began
his ascent as a two-way force, evolving from a bench role to a starter who averaged 13.1 points
in 2013–14 and earned Most Improved Player honors the following season. The team's
perseverance yielded a 48–34 record in 2013–14, good for the fourth seed in the East, as
Thibodeau's defensive system—characterized by intense help rotations and physicality—kept
opponents to 100.3 points per 100 possessions, second in the league.
Rose's partial returns in 2014–15 and 2015–16 fueled renewed playoff hopes, though injuries
persisted, limiting him to 51 games across those seasons. The Bulls posted a 50–32 mark in
2014–15, advancing to the Eastern Conference Finals before a 4–1 defeat to the Cleveland
Cavaliers, where Rose averaged 20.0 points but the team struggled offensively at 98.4 points
per game in the series. Butler's rise accelerated, culminating in his first All-Star selection in
2015, where he averaged 20.0 points and 5.8 rebounds, solidifying his role as a perimeter
defender and scorer. Thibodeau's defensive blueprint remained the core strength, with
Chicago ranking ninth in defensive rating both years, but internal tensions led to his firing after
the 2014–15 season, paving the way for Fred Hoiberg's arrival. In 2015–16, the Bulls slipped to
42–40 and missed the playoffs, yet their defensive tenacity under Thibodeau's influence had
defined an era of Eastern Conference relevance.

2016–2021: Coaching changes and continued rebuilding
Following the 2015–16 season, the Chicago Bulls traded point guard Derrick Rose, the 2011
NBA MVP and the franchise's first overall pick in 2008, to the New York Knicks on June 22,
2016, in a five-player deal that brought center Robin Lopez, guard José Calderón, and guard
Jerian Grant to Chicago. This move marked the end of the Derrick Rose era, which had
defined the team's playoff contention in the early 2010s, and signaled a shift toward
rebuilding. In conjunction with the trade, the Bulls hired Fred Hoiberg as head coach on June 2,

2015, replacing Tom Thibodeau after his dismissal, with Hoiberg signing a five-year, $25 million
contract. Under Hoiberg, the 2015–16 Bulls finished with a 42–40 record, securing the Eastern
Conference's eighth seed but exiting in the first round of the playoffs with a 4–2 loss to the
Toronto Raptors.
The 2016–17 season began with expectations of competitiveness but devolved into intentional
tanking after the Bulls traded All-Star shooting guard Jimmy Butler to the Minnesota
Timberwolves on June 22, 2017, in exchange for guard Zach LaVine, guard Kris Dunn, and the
seventh overall pick in the 2017 NBA Draft, which Chicago used to select forward Lauri
Markkanen. The trade, orchestrated by general manager Gar Forman and vice president of
basketball operations John Paxson—collectively known as "GarPax"—prioritized future assets
over immediate contention, as the Bulls finished 41–41 and missed the playoffs for the first
time since 2008. Hoiberg's tenure continued amid the rebuild, but the team's inconsistent
performance, including a 25–57 record in 2017–18, highlighted ongoing struggles with roster
integration and defensive identity. Hoiberg was fired on December 3, 2018, after a 5–19 start
to the 2018–19 season, with assistant Jim Boylen promoted as interim coach; Boylen later
guided the team to a franchise-worst 22–60 record that year, underscoring the depth of the
rebuild.
Under GarPax's leadership, the Bulls focused on the NBA Draft from 2017 to 2020 to
accumulate young talent, selecting forward Lauri Markkanen (seventh overall) in 2017, center
Wendell Carter Jr. (seventh overall) in 2018, and guard Coby White (seventh overall) in 2019, all
of whom became foundational pieces in the rebuilding core. These picks complemented the
Butler trade acquisitions, forming a youthful roster centered on developing players like
Markkanen, LaVine, and Carter Jr., though the team endured three straight seasons without
playoff appearances and a combined 69–155 record from 2017–18 to 2019–20. Fan frustration
with GarPax's direction peaked during this period, culminating in organized protests; in March
2017, supporters staged a "Fire GarPax" rally outside the United Center during a nationally
televised game, chanting for the executives' dismissal and highlighting the team's stagnation.
The discontent persisted, with fans crowdfunding a prominent West Loop billboard in July
2017 reading "Fire GarPax" to demand accountability from the front office.
Seeking stability, the Bulls hired Billy Donovan as head coach on September 22, 2020, after
his five-year stint with the Oklahoma City Thunder, where he had compiled a 243–157 record
but no playoff series wins amid that franchise's own rebuild. The 2019–20 season, shortened
to 65 games due to the COVID-19 pandemic, ended with a 22–43 record under Boylen,
positioning the Bulls 11th in the East and out of the play-in tournament. In Donovan's debut
2020–21 season, a 72-game schedule amid ongoing pandemic protocols, the Bulls improved
to 31–41 but again finished 11th in the East, missing the playoffs despite emerging as a highscoring offense led by LaVine's breakout performance. LaVine, averaging 27.4 points per
game, earned his first NBA All-Star selection in 2021, setting the stage for his expanded
leadership role in subsequent years. Key off-court developments included the 2017

completion of a 190,000-square-foot addition to the United Center, enhancing facilities for the
Bulls and enhancing long-term operational stability through ongoing lease arrangements with
the arena's ownership.

2021–2025: DeRozan-LaVine core, playoff pushes, and major trades
In the 2021 NBA offseason, the Chicago Bulls, under executive vice president of basketball
operations Artūras Karnišovas, aggressively assembled a veteran core around guard Zach
LaVine by acquiring center Nikola Vučević and forward DeMar DeRozan. On March 18, 2021,
the Bulls traded center Wendell Carter Jr., forward Otto Porter Jr., and first-round draft picks in
2021 (No. 8 overall, later Franz Wagner) and 2023 to the Orlando Magic for Vučević and
forward Al-Farouq Aminu, aiming to add an All-Star big man capable of spacing the floor with
his three-point shooting. Later, on August 6, 2021, Chicago executed a sign-and-trade with
the San Antonio Spurs, sending forward Thaddeus Young, Aminu, and a top-10 protected 2025
first-round pick to acquire DeRozan on a three-year, $85 million contract, forming a highscoring trio with LaVine and Vučević that emphasized mid-range and perimeter efficiency.
The 2021–22 season marked a resurgence for the Bulls, who finished with a 46–36 record and
secured the No. 6 seed in the Eastern Conference under coach Billy Donovan, their first 40win campaign since 2014–15. LaVine earned his second consecutive All-Star selection,
averaging 25.5 points per game, while DeRozan led the team with 27.9 points per game and
showcased elite scoring with two 50-point performances, including a career-high 56 against
the New York Knicks on January 31 and 50 in overtime versus the Los Angeles Clippers on
April 1. Despite the regular-season success, Chicago fell in five games to the Milwaukee
Bucks in the first round of the playoffs, exposing defensive vulnerabilities against elite
competition.
In 2022–23, injuries hampered the core's cohesion, but the Bulls still posted a 40–42 record
and clinched the No. 8 seed via the play-in tournament, defeating the Toronto Raptors 109–
105 before losing 91–102 to the Miami Heat. LaVine made his third straight All-Star
appearance, contributing 24.5 points per game despite missing time with a foot injury, while
DeRozan maintained his scoring dominance at 24.5 points per game on 50% shooting. The
team advanced to the playoffs but was eliminated by the Heat in five games, highlighting
ongoing issues with depth and perimeter defense.
The 2023–24 season brought further challenges, with the Bulls finishing 39–43 and again
reaching the play-in, where they upset the Atlanta Hawks 131–116 but lost 91–112 to the Heat,
missing the playoffs for the second straight year. Internal tensions surfaced amid a middling
campaign marred by injuries and inconsistent play, fueling trade rumors surrounding LaVine
and Vučević as the front office weighed contention versus rebuilding. DeRozan averaged 24.0
points per game, but the team's offensive rating ranked 17th league-wide, underscoring the
core's diminishing returns.

Entering the 2024–25 season, DeRozan departed in free agency, signing a three-year, $74
million deal with the Sacramento Kings on July 10, 2024, signaling the beginning of the core's
dissolution. The Bulls struggled early, finishing 39–43 and once more entering the play-in
without advancing, as LaVine's scoring (21.0 points per game) could not offset defensive
lapses and frontcourt inconsistencies. On February 2, 2025, at the trade deadline, Karnišovas
orchestrated a three-team deal with the Kings and San Antonio Spurs, sending LaVine and a
2028 second-round pick to Sacramento for guard Kevin Huerter, center Zach Collins, guard Tre
Jones, and a 2025 first-round pick (top-14 protected), reuniting LaVine with DeRozan while
acquiring younger rotation pieces without additional draft capital. This move, part of a broader
pivot to youth under Karnišovas's direction, traded immediate star power for developmental
assets, ending the DeRozan-LaVine era after three playoff appearances but no series
wins.

2025–present: Youth movement and early success
Following the major trades at the conclusion of the 2024–25 season, including the February
2025 deal that sent Zach LaVine to the Sacramento Kings in exchange for Kevin Huerter and a
2028 second-round pick, the Chicago Bulls pivoted toward a youth-driven roster. This shift
built on prior acquisitions like Josh Giddey, obtained in a 2024 draft-night trade from the
Oklahoma City Thunder, and Matas Buzelis, selected 11th overall in the 2024 NBA Draft. The
front office, led by executive vice president Artūras Karnišovas, retained core young talents
such as Coby White and Ayo Dosunmu while emphasizing player development over immediate

As of February 2026, the Bulls' future draft capital supported this long-term strategy, including

In the 2025 offseason, the Bulls focused on stability and depth, re-signing Giddey to a fouryear contract extension and adding pieces like guard Tre Jones on an extension and forward
Isaac Okoro via a trade involving Lonzo Ball. Huerter's integration provided veteran shooting
to complement the emerging core, averaging 12.9 points per game early in the season with a
49.2% field goal percentage. Nikola Vučević remained a steady presence at center, posting
19.3 points and 12.0 rebounds per game through the first seven contests, anchoring the
frontcourt without any trade discussions materializing. This approach validated Karnišovas's
long-term strategy, which had roots in earlier rebuild efforts but now prioritized internal
growth.

The 2025–26 season opened with surprising success, as the Bulls achieved a 6–1 record by
early November, including victories over the Detroit Pistons and Philadelphia 76ers (113–111 on
November 4). This marked Chicago's best start since a 12–0 run in 1996–97, propelled by
defensive improvements and balanced scoring. Buzelis, in his second NBA year, emerged as
a breakout performer, averaging 14.9 points and 4.6 rebounds per game while scoring in
double figures in five straight outings, including a 27-point explosion against the Sacramento
Kings earlier in the season. Giddey's playmaking shone brightest, with back-to-back tripledoubles—the first for a Bull since Michael Jordan in 1989—positioning him as a Most Improved
Player candidate and fueling the team's transition offense.
Amid the hot streak, the Bulls made a minor roster adjustment on November 2, assigning
rookie forward Noa Essengue to their G League affiliate, the Windy City Bulls, to aid his
development without disrupting the rotation. Dosunmu and White contributed steadily in
the backcourt, with Dosunmu's defensive versatility helping limit opponents to under 105
points in four of the first seven games. These early defensive gains, paired with the young
core's cohesion, suggested a potential avoidance of the lottery for the first time in years,
affirming the rebuild's progress under coach Billy Donovan. As of November 18, 2025, the Bulls
stood at 7–6 following a 130–127 road victory over the Denver Nuggets on November 17,
which ended a five-game losing streak after their strong opening.
