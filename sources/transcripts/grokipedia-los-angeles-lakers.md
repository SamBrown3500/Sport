The Los Angeles Lakers are an American professional basketball team competing in the
National Basketball Association (NBA) as a member of the league's Western Conference
Pacific Division. Founded in 1947 as the Minneapolis Lakers in the National Basketball
League (NBL), the franchise joined the NBA in 1949 after the league's merger with the
Basketball Association of America (BAA) and relocated to Los Angeles in 1960, where it has
played ever since. With 17 NBA championships—the second-most in league history behind
the Boston Celtics—the Lakers are one of the most storied franchises in professional sports,
known for their global fanbase, iconic purple-and-gold uniforms, and home games at
Crypto.com Arena.
The team's early success came in Minneapolis, where it captured five championships in the
late 1940s and early 1950s, led by Hall of Famer George Mikan, the NBA's first superstar who
revolutionized the game as a dominant center. After struggling post-relocation due to
frequent finals losses to the Celtics, the Lakers entered their Showtime era in the 1980s under
coach Pat Riley, featuring Magic Johnson and Kareem Abdul-Jabbar, resulting in five titles
(1980, 1982, 1985, 1987, 1988) through fast-paced, entertaining play that defined the
franchise's Hollywood image. The 2000s brought another dominant run with the trio of Kobe
Bryant, Shaquille O'Neal, and coach Phil Jackson, securing three consecutive championships
(2000–2002) and two more in 2009–2010, followed by a 2020 title led by LeBron James and
Anthony Davis amid the COVID-19 pandemic.
The Lakers also hold an all-time record of 3,653 wins and 2,515 losses as of September 2026
(regular season only), with 66 playoff appearances and numerous Hall of Famers including
Elgin Baylor, Jerry West, Wilt Chamberlain, and more recent stars like Bryant, James, and Luka
Dončić. Ownership transitioned in October 2025 when the Buss family sold a majority stake
to Guggenheim Partners CEO Mark Walter for a $10 billion valuation, approved by the NBA
Board of Governors. In August 2026, Walter agreed to sell the majority stake to Joshua
Kushner and Bob Iger at a $12.5 billion valuation, pending NBA Board of Governors approval,
while Jeanie Buss petitioned the court to retain the family's remaining minority stake.
The franchise's cultural significance extends to retired jerseys for 13 legends and a legacy of
innovation, from pioneering television broadcasts to fostering rivalries that have shaped the
NBA.

History


Minneapolis era (1946–1960)
The franchise originated as the Detroit Gems in the National Basketball League (NBL) in 1946,
owned by C. King Boring and coached by Joel Mason, but the team struggled with a dismal 440 record in its only season, drawing poor attendance in Detroit. In February 1947, local
businessmen Ben Berger and Morris Chalfen purchased the franchise for $15,000 and
relocated it to Minneapolis, renaming it the Lakers to honor Minnesota's "Land of 10,000
Lakes." The team drafted DePaul University star George Mikan second overall in the inaugural
BAA dispersal draft and hired John Kundla as coach, setting the stage for immediate success
in the 1947-48 NBL season, where they finished 43-17 and won the league championship.
George Mikan, a 6-foot-10 center known for his hook shot and rebounding prowess, became
the cornerstone of the Lakers, leading them to five championships and revolutionizing
professional basketball by forcing rule changes like the widening of the free-throw lane from 6
to 12 feet in 1951 to limit his dominance under the basket. Mikan won three scoring titles
from 1949 to 1951, averaging over 23 points per game in those seasons, and earned four AllStar selections while serving as player-coach in his final years. Supported by Hall of Famers
like forward Vern Mikkelsen and Jim Pollard, the Lakers captured NBL titles in 1948 and 1949
before the league merged with the Basketball Association of America to form the NBA in
1949-50. In the NBA, they defeated the Washington Capitols in the 1949 Finals (swept 2-0)
and the Syracuse Nationals 4-2 in the 1950 Finals, where low-scoring games highlighted
stalling tactics employed against Mikan's interior presence. The dynasty peaked with threepeat championships in 1952 (4-3 over New York Knicks), 1953 (4-1 over Nationals), and 1954
(4-3 over Nationals), compiling a 243-114 regular-season record from 1948-1954 and
establishing the Lakers as the NBA's premier team.
A pivotal event underscoring the era's pacing issues occurred in the 1954 Finals, when the
Nationals used deliberate stalling to secure a 71-65 victory in Game 7, the lowest-scoring NBA
Finals game ever at the time, which contributed to the league's adoption of the 24-second
shot clock for the 1954-55 season to combat such tactics and boost excitement. Mikan's
retirement in 1956 after a knee-injured 1955-56 season (33-38 record, first playoff miss since
1948) marked the beginning of decline, as the team integrated rookies like Clyde Lovellette
but struggled with attendance drops and roster instability. From 1954-1960, the Lakers posted
a 185-243 regular-season record, making just one playoff appearance in 1959 (lost 0-2 to
Boston Celtics), reflecting the challenges of transitioning without their star amid growing
competition from eastern teams.

Move to Los Angeles and rivalry with Celtics (1960–1973)

In 1960, the Lakers relocated from Minneapolis to Los Angeles under owner Bob Short, who
had acquired the franchise in 1957 amid declining attendance and financial difficulties in their
previous market. The move marked the NBA's first expansion to the West Coast, aiming to
tap into the growing entertainment hub of Southern California, though initial fan turnout was
modest as the team adapted to a new audience. The Lakers played their early home games
at the Los Angeles Memorial Sports Arena, a newly opened venue in 1959 that hosted the
team until 1967, but logistical challenges like shared scheduling with other events and the
arena's modest capacity of around 16,000 limited early momentum. Short sold the team in
1965 to Jack Kent Cooke for $5 million, who oversaw the transition to the more luxurious
Forum in Inglewood starting in the 1967-68 season.
The Lakers built their foundation around star forwards Elgin Baylor and Jerry West, forming one
of the league's most dynamic duos. Baylor, selected first overall in the 1958 NBA Draft by the
Minneapolis Lakers, transitioned seamlessly to the West Coast and earned the 1959 Rookie of
the Year award after averaging 24.9 points and 15.0 rebounds per game in his debut season.
18 West joined as the second overall pick in the 1960 Draft, quickly emerging as a scoring
sensation and winning NBA scoring titles in 1965-66 (31.0 points per game) and 1969-70 (31.2
points per game), while earning 10 All-NBA First Team selections during the era. Together,
Baylor and West led the Lakers to seven NBA Finals appearances between 1962 and 1970,
though the team fell short of a championship in each, posting records of 4-3 losses to the
Boston Celtics in 1962, 1963, 1965, 1966, 1968, and 1969, and a 4-3 defeat to the New York
Knicks in 1970.
The Lakers' acquisition of center Wilt Chamberlain via trade from the Philadelphia 76ers in July
1968—for Darrall Imhoff, Archie Clark, and Jerry Chambers—added unparalleled size and
rebounding to the roster, boosting their regular-season dominance with a 55-27 record in
Chamberlain's debut year. However, injuries hampered the trio's synergy, as seen in the
1969 Finals where West's 42.8 points per game earned him the inaugural Finals MVP despite
the 4-3 loss to Boston, and in the 1970 Finals where Chamberlain's knee injury limited him
during the Knicks defeat. Chamberlain's defensive presence proved pivotal in the 1971-72
season, where he averaged 14.8 rebounds and focused on playmaking en route to the Lakers'
first Los Angeles-era title, though a 4-1 Finals rematch loss to the Knicks in 1973 underscored
ongoing postseason vulnerabilities.
The relocation amplified the Lakers' storied rivalry with the Boston Celtics, rooted in the
Minneapolis era but exploding in Los Angeles through repeated Finals clashes dominated by
Boston's Bill Russell. From 1962 to 1969, the Celtics won all seven series against the Lakers,
with Russell's revolutionary shot-blocking—averaging 22.5 rebounds per game in those Finals
—repeatedly thwarting Baylor's acrobatic drives and West's mid-range mastery, including a
iconic block on West's layup in the final seconds of the 1969 Game 7. By 1973, the
franchises had met in 12 NBA Finals overall, establishing the league's premier rivalry and

symbolizing East Coast grit versus West Coast glamour, though Boston's defensive edge under
Russell prevented Lakers success until his retirement.

Showtime era (1973–1991)
The Showtime era of the Los Angeles Lakers began with the acquisition of Kareem AbdulJabbar via trade from the Milwaukee Bucks on June 16, 1975, in exchange for Elmore Smith,
Brian Winters, Junior Bridgeman, and Dave Meyers. Abdul-Jabbar, already a six-time NBA
MVP, anchored the Lakers' frontcourt and earned his third MVP award in the 1975-76 season,
averaging 27.7 points and 16.9 rebounds per game while leading Los Angeles to a 53-29
record. His skyhook shot and defensive prowess formed the foundation for the team's
transition to a high-octane, fast-break style known as Showtime, which emphasized transition
scoring and no-look passes over traditional half-court sets.
The arrival of Earvin "Magic" Johnson as the first overall pick in the 1979 NBA Draft
revolutionized the Lakers' offense, pairing his visionary passing and athleticism with AbdulJabbar's scoring efficiency. Johnson, selected after the Lakers acquired the pick from the
New Orleans Jazz, averaged 18.0 points and 7.7 assists as a rookie, propelling Los Angeles to
the 1980 NBA Finals against the Philadelphia 76ers. In Game 6 with Abdul-Jabbar sidelined
by injury, the 20-year-old Johnson volunteered to play center, delivering a Finals MVP
performance of 42 points, 15 rebounds, and 7 assists in a 123-107 clincher, securing the
Lakers' first championship since 1972.
Under coach Pat Riley, who took over in 1981 after Paul Westhead's midseason dismissal, the
Lakers refined their Showtime system into a dynasty, winning four more championships in the
1980s. Riley's tenure from 1981 to 1990 produced a 533-194 regular-season record and
nine Finals appearances, including victories over Philadelphia in 1982 (4-2), Boston in 1985 (42), and Detroit in 1987 (4-2) and 1988 (4-3). The nickname "Showtime" originated from
Lakers broadcaster Chick Hearn, who first used it during a 1979-80 game to describe the
team's exhilarating, entertainment-focused play. Riley's emphasis on conditioning and
pressure defense complemented the offense, resulting in the Lakers earning first-round byes
in the playoffs as Pacific Division champions each year from 1980 to 1989, often dispatching
opponents in decisive "bye-bye" fashion during subsequent rounds.
Roster evolution bolstered the core duo, with the 1982 draft selection of forward James
Worthy first overall adding a dynamic finisher who averaged 13.4 points as a rookie and
contributed to three championships. Worthy's fast-break dunks and clutch playoff scoring—
highlighted by his 1988 Finals MVP award with 36 points in Game 7—exemplified Showtime's
flair, while role players like Michael Cooper provided elite perimeter defense. This blend of
star power and system play delivered five titles overall, cementing the era as a pinnacle of NBA
entertainment and success.

Post-Showtime struggles (1991–1996)


The retirement of Magic Johnson on November 7, 1991, following his announcement that he
had tested positive for HIV, marked the symbolic end of the Lakers' dominant Showtime era
and delivered a profound emotional blow to the franchise, its players, and fans. Johnson, the
architect of five NBA championships and the heart of the team's high-flying style, held a press
conference where he stated, "Because of the HIV virus that I have obtained, I will have to retire
today from the Lakers," leaving the organization reeling from the loss of its iconic leader. The
announcement not only stunned the basketball world but also prompted widespread grief
among Lakers supporters, who viewed Johnson as synonymous with the team's identity,
exacerbating the sense of uncertainty as the franchise transitioned without its cornerstone
player.
In the immediate aftermath, the Lakers struggled to maintain competitiveness, posting
mediocre records amid frequent coaching instability and ineffective roster moves. Under head
coach Mike Dunleavy, who had led the team since 1990, the 1991–92 season ended with a 46–
36 record and a first-round playoff loss to the Portland Trail Blazers in five games, signaling
early decline without Johnson's presence. Dunleavy departed after the season, replaced by
assistant Randy Pfund in 1992, but the team's fortunes worsened dramatically; the 1992–93
campaign yielded a league-worst 27–55 record, followed by an identical 27–55 mark in 1993–
94, resulting in back-to-back missed playoffs and the franchise's worst two-year stretch since
the early 1950s. Pfund was fired midway through the 1993–94 season, giving way to Del
Harris, yet the persistent underperformance highlighted deeper organizational challenges in
adapting post-Showtime.
Rebuilding efforts faltered through misguided draft selections and acquisitions that failed to
restore contention, prolonging the playoff drought that spanned from 1994 to 1995 before a
brief resurgence. The Lakers selected point guard Nick Van Exel 37th overall in the 1993 NBA
Draft, a second-round pick who showed promise with his scoring and playmaking but could
not elevate the team amid inconsistent supporting talent. Similarly, forward Cedric Ceballos
was acquired from the Phoenix Suns in August 1994 in exchange for a future first-round pick
(later used to select Michael Finley); while Ceballos averaged 21.1 points per game in his first
Lakers season, injuries and team dysfunction limited his impact, contributing to another firstround exit in 1994–95 despite a 45–37 record. These moves exemplified the front office's
struggles to identify and develop foundational pieces, as high draft assets yielded short-term
contributors rather than long-term solutions, culminating in a six-year span of irrelevance by
mid-decade.
The period's turnaround began in the 1996 offseason, as general manager Jerry West
orchestrated pivotal acquisitions to inject star power and end the malaise. On June 26, 1996,
the Lakers traded center Vlade Divac to the Charlotte Hornets for the 13th overall draft pick,
high school phenom Kobe Bryant, whose raw potential West coveted despite Divac's

established role as a reliable starter. Just weeks later, on July 18, 1996, free-agent center
Shaquille O'Neal signed a groundbreaking seven-year, $120 million contract with Los Angeles,
leaving the Orlando Magic and bringing dominant interior presence to pair with emerging
talent. These moves, executed amid the 1995–96 season's relative improvement to 53–29 and
a Western Conference Semifinals appearance, positioned the franchise for revival after years
of stagnation.

Shaquille O'Neal and Kobe Bryant dynasty (1996–2004)
The Los Angeles Lakers initiated a transformative era in 1996 by securing two cornerstone
players who would define the franchise's success for the next decade. On July 18, 1996, the
Lakers signed free agent center Shaquille O'Neal to a seven-year, $120 million contract after
he declined to re-sign with the Orlando Magic, bringing unparalleled dominance to the paint
with his size, power, and scoring ability. Just weeks earlier, on June 26, 1996, the Charlotte
Hornets selected 17-year-old guard Kobe Bryant 13th overall in the NBA Draft before trading
his rights to the Lakers on draft night in exchange for center Vlade Divac, a move orchestrated
by general manager Jerry West to pair the young phenom with O'Neal. Initially, the duo's
partnership was marked by tension and unrefined chemistry; O'Neal, the established superstar,
viewed the precocious Bryant as overly eager and undisciplined, leading to public spats, oncourt disagreements over shot selection, and even physical altercations during practices
under coaches Del Harris and Kurt Rambis from 1996 to 1999. Despite reaching the
Western Conference Finals in 1998 and 1999, these internal conflicts contributed to playoff
shortcomings, including losses to the Utah Jazz and San Antonio Spurs.
The arrival of Phil Jackson as head coach in the summer of 1999 marked a pivotal shift,
introducing discipline and a sophisticated system that harnessed the Shaq-Kobe tandem.
Hired on a five-year, $30 million deal after leading the Chicago Bulls to six championships,
Jackson implemented the triangle offense—a fluid, read-and-react scheme emphasizing ball
movement, spacing, and post play that he had perfected with Michael Jordan and Scottie
Pippen. Though Bryant initially resisted the system's emphasis on collective decisionmaking, viewing it as a constraint on his improvisational style, Jackson's psychological
approach and mediation helped forge synergy, allowing O'Neal to dominate inside while Bryant
thrived on the perimeter. This tactical evolution, supported by key role players like guard
Derek Fisher, whose steady shooting and clutch play stabilized the backcourt, and forward
Rick Fox, who provided versatile defense and timely scoring, propelled the Lakers to
unparalleled success.
Under Jackson's guidance, the Lakers embarked on a three-year championship dynasty from
2000 to 2002, becoming the first team since the Bulls to achieve a three-peat. In the 2000
NBA Finals, they defeated the Indiana Pacers 4-2, with O'Neal earning Finals MVP honors after
averaging 38 points and 16.7 rebounds per game, including a 40-point, 24-rebound
performance in Game 1; he also captured the regular-season MVP award that year. The

2001 Finals saw the Lakers sweep the Philadelphia 76ers 4-0, as O'Neal again claimed Finals
MVP with averages of 33 points and 15.8 rebounds, highlighted by a 40-point, 20-rebound
effort in Game 2 despite a toe injury. The dynasty culminated in 2022 with a 4-0 sweep of
the New Jersey Nets, where O'Neal secured his third consecutive Finals MVP, posting 36.3
points and 12.3 rebounds per game, while Bryant's perimeter scoring complemented the
interior assault. These victories, fueled by the triangle's efficiency and the stars' growing
rapport, established the Lakers as the NBA's preeminent power.
The dynasty unraveled in the 2004 NBA Finals, where the Lakers fell 4-1 to the Detroit Pistons
in a stunning upset that exposed vulnerabilities in aging stars and internal discord. Despite
adding veterans Karl Malone and Gary Payton, the physical, team-oriented Pistons neutralized
O'Neal's dominance through collective defense led by Ben Wallace and Rasheed Wallace,
limiting the Lakers' offense to stagnant play. The loss exacerbated fractures between O'Neal
and Bryant, culminating in O'Neal's trade to the Miami Heat on July 14, 2004, for Lamar Odom,
Caron Butler, Brian Grant, and a future first-round pick, effectively ending the partnership that
had delivered three titles in four years.

Kobe Bryant solo era (2004–2016)
The departure of Shaquille O'Neal to the Miami Heat on July 14, 2004, in exchange for Lamar
Odom, Caron Butler, Brian Grant, and a future first-round draft pick, signaled the end of the
Lakers' dynasty with the center and ushered in Kobe Bryant's era as the franchise's primary
leader. Under new head coach Rudy Tomjanovich, who resigned after 41 games due to
health issues, and interim coach Frank Hamblen, the Lakers struggled with a 34-48 record in
the 2004-05 season, missing the playoffs for the first time since 1993. Phil Jackson
returned as head coach on June 15, 2005, on a one-year contract, bringing stability and
guiding the team to incremental improvements, though they still fell short of the postseason in
2005-06 with a 45-37 record.
Bryant's individual brilliance defined the early years of this period, culminating in his iconic 81point performance against the Toronto Raptors on January 22, 2006, the highest scoring
output in NBA history since Wilt Chamberlain's 100 points in 1962, leading the Lakers to a 122104 victory. He captured the NBA scoring title in the 2005-06 season with a league-leading
35.4 points per game and repeated the feat in 2006-07, averaging 31.6 points per game amid
ongoing team challenges, including a first-round playoff sweep by the Phoenix Suns in 2006.
48 These seasons highlighted Bryant's scoring prowess and resilience, as he carried a
rebuilding roster featuring emerging talents like Lamar Odom and a young Andrew Bynum,
though the Lakers managed only one playoff appearance in 2007, losing again to the Suns in
the first round.
A pivotal shift occurred on February 1, 2008, when the Lakers acquired Pau Gasol from the
Memphis Grizzlies in exchange for Kwame Brown, Javaris Crittenton, Aaron McKie, the draft

rights to Marc Gasol, and two future first-round picks, forming a dynamic duo with Bryant that
revitalized the franchise. Under Jackson's leadership, the bolstered lineup achieved a 57-25
record in 2007-08, reaching the NBA Finals but falling to the Boston Celtics in six games. The
partnership proved championship-caliber in 2009, as the Lakers defeated the Orlando Magic
4-1 in the Finals, with Bryant earning Finals MVP honors after averaging 32.4 points, 5.6
rebounds, and 7.4 assists per game. Jackson secured his 10th title as a coach, and the
Lakers repeated in 2010, overcoming the Celtics in a seven-game series for a 4-3 victory,
where Bryant again claimed Finals MVP with 28.6 points and 8.0 rebounds per game, marking
the franchise's 16th championship.
Jackson retired after the 2011 Finals loss to the Dallas Mavericks, ending his second stint with
five more championships and a 336-124 regular-season record from 2005-11. The Lakers
hired Mike Brown as head coach on June 20, 2011, on a four-year deal, but his tenure lasted
only five games into the lockout-shortened 2011-12 season, as the team fired him after a 4125 start amid defensive inconsistencies and a second-round playoff exit. Mike D'Antoni
succeeded him on November 12, 2012, on a three-year contract with a team option,
emphasizing an up-tempo offense that suited Bryant's style but yielded mixed results,
including a Western Conference semifinals loss to the San Antonio Spurs in 2013 before
D'Antoni resigned on April 30, 2014. Byron Scott was appointed head coach on July 28,
2014, on a four-year, $17 million deal, aiming to rebuild around an aging Bryant while
integrating younger players like Jordan Clarkson and D'Angelo Russell.
Post-2010, the Lakers endured a sharp decline due to injuries, particularly to Bryant, who
missed significant time in 2013-14 and played only 35 games in 2014-15 because of a torn
Achilles and fractured knee. The team posted losing records in four consecutive seasons from
2011-12 to 2014-15, failing to advance past the first round after 2013. Bryant's legacy
concluded on April 13, 2016, when he scored 60 points in his final game, a 101-96 win over the
Utah Jazz, retiring with five NBA championships, 18 All-Star selections, and status as the
Lakers' all-time leading scorer. The 2015-16 season encapsulated the era's struggles,
finishing with a franchise-worst 17-65 record under Scott, the lowest winning percentage
(.207) in Lakers history and marking their third straight season without playoffs.

Post-Kobe transition (2016–2018)
Following Kobe Bryant's retirement after the 2015–16 season, the Los Angeles Lakers shifted
focus to a youth-driven rebuild, prioritizing draft selections and player development over
immediate contention. In April 2016, the team hired Luke Walton, a former Golden State
Warriors assistant coach and Lakers player from 2003 to 2012, as their new head coach on a
multi-year deal. Walton, who had helped lead the Warriors to a 39–4 interim record earlier that
year, emphasized a fast-paced, space-oriented offensive system inspired by his time under
Steve Kerr, aiming to foster creativity and ball movement among emerging talents.

The Lakers' rebuilding efforts gained momentum through the draft, selecting Duke forward
Brandon Ingram with the second overall pick in 2016, a versatile scorer with exceptional length
at 6-foot-9. Ingram quickly became a cornerstone, averaging 9.4 points as a rookie while
showcasing his perimeter shooting and defensive potential. In 2017, under new president of
basketball operations Magic Johnson—hired in February after the dismissal of general manager
Mitch Kupchak—the team added UCLA point guard Lonzo Ball at No. 2 overall and Utah
forward Kyle Kuzma at No. 27, forming a promising young core alongside holdover forward
Julius Randle. Johnson, a Hall of Famer and five-time Lakers champion, oversaw personnel
decisions from 2017 to 2019, emphasizing long-term growth over quick fixes. Randle, entering
his fourth season, emerged as a double-double threat with 16.1 points and 8.0 rebounds, while
Kuzma impressed as a rookie scorer at 12.7 points per game.
Despite the influx of talent, the Lakers endured challenging seasons, finishing 26–56 in 2016–
17 and improving slightly to 35–47 in 2017–18, missing the playoffs both years and prioritizing
development over wins. Walton's pace-and-space approach yielded glimpses of potential,
with the team ranking 10th in pace during 2017–18, but defensive inconsistencies and injuries
hampered progress. The front office's aggressive pursuits in free agency fell short, as targets
like DeMarcus Cousins signed elsewhere in 2017 and Paul George re-signed with Oklahoma
City in 2018, leaving cap space intact but underscoring the need for a marquee addition.
These misses ultimately positioned the Lakers to sign LeBron James in July 2018, marking the
end of the transitional phase.

LeBron James arrival and early years (2018–2020)
In July 2018, LeBron James, a four-time NBA Most Valuable Player, signed a four-year contract
worth $153.3 million with the Los Angeles Lakers as a free agent, marking his return to the
franchise where his career had begun in a different capacity and aiming to revitalize the team
after several rebuilding years. The signing brought immediate excitement but also pressure,
as James averaged 27.4 points, 8.5 rebounds, and 8.3 assists per game in his Lakers debut
season. However, the 2018–19 Lakers struggled with cohesion and depth, finishing with a 37–
45 record and missing the playoffs for the sixth consecutive year, hampered by a midseason
groin injury to James that sidelined him for 17 games and contributed to a late-season
collapse.
Following the disappointing season, the Lakers front office underwent changes, with Magic
Johnson resigning as president of basketball operations in April 2019, paving the way for a
more aggressive roster overhaul. In January 2019, Anthony Davis, the New Orleans Pelicans'
All-Star center, publicly requested a trade, listing the Lakers among his preferred destinations,
which intensified trade rumors and led to a league investigation into potential tampering by
Lakers management. On June 15, 2019, the Lakers acquired Davis in a blockbuster deal,
sending Lonzo Ball, Brandon Ingram, Josh Hart, and three first-round draft picks (including the
Nos. and 17 picks in 2019) to New Orleans, pairing James with a dominant big man to form a

potent frontcourt duo. To lead the revamped roster, the Lakers hired Frank Vogel as head
coach on May 13, 2019, to a three-year contract; Vogel, known for his defensive expertise from
stints with the Indiana Pacers and Orlando Magic, implemented schemes emphasizing rim
protection through Davis, disciplined perimeter rotations, and switchable lineups that
transformed the Lakers into the NBA's top defensive team, allowing just 104.3 points per 100
possessions during the regular season.
The 2019–20 season began promisingly for the Lakers, who started 17–4, but was derailed by
injuries to key supporting players, including a preseason ACL tear for center DeMarcus Cousins
that wiped out his entire debut campaign and multiple absences for point guard Rajon Rondo
due to back and hand issues, forcing reliance on a thinned rotation amid the league's
suspension in March 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Despite these setbacks, James
and Davis remained largely available, with James playing 67 games and Davis 62, helping the
team finish 52–19 (.732 winning percentage) in the shortened 72-game schedule before
resuming in the NBA Bubble at Walt Disney World. In the playoffs, the Lakers dominated,
defeating the Portland Trail Blazers, Houston Rockets, and Denver Nuggets to reach the Finals,
where they overcame the Miami Heat 4–2 to win the championship on October 11, 2020—the
franchise's 17th title and first since 2010. James earned Finals MVP honors with averages of
29.8 points, 11.8 rebounds, and 8.5 assists per game, securing his fourth NBA championship
ring.
Vogel's defensive system proved pivotal in the Bubble, holding opponents to under 100 points
in five of six Finals games, while role players like forward Kyle Kuzma and guard Alex Caruso
provided crucial energy and versatility; Kuzma contributed 10.8 points per game in the
postseason with timely scoring and rebounding, including a 21-point effort in Game 3 of the
Western Conference semifinals, and Caruso offered elite perimeter defense and hustle plays,
averaging 5.9 points and 2.4 steals per 36 minutes while stepping into the starting lineup
during injuries. This championship laid the foundation for the James-Davis partnership's
sustained success in subsequent years.

LeBron James and Anthony Davis championships (2020–2025)
Following their 2020 NBA championship victory, the Los Angeles Lakers, led by LeBron James
and Anthony Davis, maintained a competitive presence in the Western Conference through a
mix of playoff appearances, individual milestones, and roster adjustments amid persistent
injury challenges. The duo's core remained the foundation, with James providing veteran
leadership and scoring prowess while Davis excelled defensively, but the team navigated
significant turnover and coaching changes without securing another title.
In the 2020-21 season, the defending champions acquired point guard Russell Westbrook via
a sign-and-trade deal with the Washington Wizards in August 2021, sending Kyle Kuzma,
Kentavious Caldwell-Pope, Montrezl Harrell, and a 2021 first-round pick in return. However,

injuries limited the team's cohesion, resulting in a seventh-seed finish and a first-round playoff
loss to the Phoenix Suns in six games, 4-2. The Suns outscored the Lakers by an average of
8.5 points per game in the series, highlighting defensive vulnerabilities exposed without a fully
healthy roster. Westbrook was later traded in February 2023 to the Utah Jazz as part of a
three-team deal that brought back D'Angelo Russell, Malik Beasley, and Jarred Vanderbilt from
the Minnesota Timberwolves.
Under new head coach Darvin Ham, hired in June 2022 after serving as an assistant with the
Milwaukee Bucks, the Lakers rebounded in the 2022-23 season, finishing seventh in the West
and advancing through the play-in tournament to reach the Western Conference Finals. Ham's
tenure emphasized defensive intensity and James-Davis synergy, leading to a 16-7 playoff run
before a 4-0 sweep by the Denver Nuggets, where Nikola Jokić averaged 30.0 points and 13.5
rebounds. During this period, James achieved a historic milestone on February 7, 2023,
breaking Kareem Abdul-Jabbar's all-time NBA scoring record with 38,390 points in a game
against the Oklahoma City Thunder. Davis, meanwhile, peaked defensively, earning AllDefensive First Team honors in 2020 and again in 2024, while leading the league in blocks
during the 2023 playoffs with 1.8 per game. Roster changes included the 2023 NBA Draft
selection of guard Jalen Hood-Schifino at No. 17 overall from Indiana University, adding young
backcourt depth amid ongoing turnover.
Ham's coaching stint ended after the 2023-24 season, during which the Lakers won the
inaugural NBA In-Season Tournament but exited the playoffs in the first round with a 4-1 loss to
the Nuggets, prompting his dismissal in May 2024 after a 90-74 regular-season record over
two years. The 2024-25 campaign under new coach JJ Redick saw the Lakers post a 50-32
record, securing the third seed in the Western Conference and demonstrating improved
balance around James and Davis, though the season was impacted mid-way by a blockbuster
trade in February 2025 that sent Davis, Max Christie, Jalen Hood-Schifino, a 2025 first-round
pick, and a 2025 second-round pick to the Dallas Mavericks in exchange for Luka Dončić, Maxi
Kleber, and Markieff Morris (with the Utah Jazz facilitating for a protected 2027 second-round
pick from Dallas), ending the James-Davis partnership after 76 games together that year.

Luka Dončić era begins (2025–present)
In February 2025, the Los Angeles Lakers executed a blockbuster three-team trade to acquire
Luka Dončić from the Dallas Mavericks, marking a pivotal shift toward a new superstar-led era.
The Lakers received Dončić, Maxi Kleber, and Markieff Morris, while sending Anthony Davis,
Max Christie, Jalen Hood-Schifino, a 2025 first-round draft pick, and a 2025 second-round
pick to the Mavericks; the Utah Jazz were involved to facilitate salary matching and received a

protected 2027 second-round pick from Dallas. This move paired the 25-year-old
Slovenian phenom with LeBron James, aiming to blend Dončić's playmaking prowess with
James's veteran leadership for sustained contention.
Dončić's integration into the Lakers' system under head coach JJ Redick, who entered his
second season after a contract extension in September 2025, has been seamless from the
outset of the 2025–26 campaign. In the first five games, Dončić averaged 19.6 points,
6.2 rebounds, and 3.8 assists per game, contributing to key victories, including a 130–120 win
over the Miami Heat on November 3 where he tallied 29 points, 11 rebounds, and 10 assists,
and a 118–116 triumph against the San Antonio Spurs on November 5. His synergy
with James, who returned from an early-season injury, has emphasized pick-and-roll actions
and off-ball movement, allowing Dončić to orchestrate the offense while James focuses on
finishing and spacing.
The Lakers' early success, with a 10–4 record as of November 16, 2025, has been bolstered by
defensive enhancements from offseason additions DeAndre Ayton and Marcus Smart. Ayton,
signed as a free agent, provides rim protection and rebounding, while Smart's perimeter
defense has limited opponents' three-point shooting to under 35% in wins against Miami and
San Antonio. These moves have elevated the team's defensive rating to 115.9 (18th in
the NBA), a modest improvement from the prior season's 113.8, particularly in transition stops
and paint protection.
