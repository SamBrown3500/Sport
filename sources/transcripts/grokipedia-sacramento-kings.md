The Sacramento Kings are a professional basketball franchise competing in the National
Basketball Association (NBA) as a member of the league's Western Conference Pacific
Division. Founded in 1948 as the Rochester Royals after origins in the National Basketball
League (NBL) dating back to 1945, the team is one of the oldest continuously operating
franchises in major professional North American sports. The franchise has experienced
multiple relocations and name changes, including stints as the Cincinnati Royals (1957–1972)
and Kansas City-Omaha/Kansas City Kings (1972–1985), before relocating to Sacramento in
1985, where it has remained ever since.
Throughout its history, the Kings have achieved one NBA championship in 1951 (as the
Rochester Royals) and multiple division titles in 1949, 1952, 1979, 2002, 2003, and 2023.
The team has made 30 playoff appearances, with notable deep runs including Western
Conference Finals berths in 1981 and 2002, highlighted by their franchise-best regular-season
record of 61–21 during the 2001–02 season. Iconic players have defined various eras, such
as Hall of Famers Oscar Robertson (who led the Royals to the 1961–64 division titles), Nate
"Tiny" Archibald, Vlade Divac, and Chris Webber, as well as Mitch Richmond and Peja
Stojaković, contributing to the team's legacy and the retirement of numbers like 1 (Archibald),
2 (Richmond), 4 (Webber), 14 (Robertson), 16 (Stojaković), and 21 (Divac).
The Kings play their home games at the Golden 1 Center, a state-of-the-art arena in downtown
Sacramento that opened in 2016 and serves as a hub for the team's vibrant community
engagement. Under general manager Scott Perry, hired in April 2025, and head coach Doug
Christie (permanent since May 2025, following Mike Brown's tenure), the franchise has
emphasized rebuilding through roster adjustments and youth development. Notable recent
changes include the trade of De'Aaron Fox to the San Antonio Spurs for Zach LaVine in
February 2025, along with the additions of veterans DeMar DeRozan, Russell Westbrook, and
Dennis Schröder. Ongoing stars include Domantas Sabonis, sidelined since November with a
knee injury, and Keegan Murray, out with an ankle injury. The team secured a Pacific Division
title in 2023 but missed the 2025 playoffs. As of January 2026 in the 2025–26 season,
following a three-game winning streak over the Houston Rockets, Los Angeles Lakers, and
New York Knicks, the Kings hold an 11–30 record, with poor offensive and defensive efficiency,
rebounding, and true shooting percentage.

History


Rochester Royals era (1945–1957)
The Rochester Royals franchise was established in 1945 by brothers Les Harrison and Jack
Harrison, who sponsored the team upon its entry into the National Basketball League (NBL) as
an expansion franchise based in Rochester, New York. The team adopted the "Royals" name
following an essay contest won by 15-year-old local resident Richard Paeth, reflecting the
city's historical ties to the Rochester Royals minor league baseball team. Coached by owner
Les Harrison, the Royals assembled a competitive roster featuring guard Al Cervi, a defensive
specialist and explosive scorer who joined after military service, along with guard Bob
Davies and other early contributors like Red Holzman. This founding marked the beginning
of the franchise's roots in professional basketball amid the post-World War II growth of the
sport.
In their debut 1945–46 NBL season, the Royals achieved immediate success, finishing second
in the Eastern Division with a 24–10 record before sweeping the Sheboygan Red Skins 3–0 in
the finals to claim the league championship. Davies emerged as a star, earning All-NBL First
Team honors, while Cervi contributed significantly with his perimeter defense and scoring. The
team repeated as division champions in 1946–47 and 1947–48, posting the league's best
regular-season record in the latter year at 44–16, though they fell 3–1 to the Minneapolis
Lakers in the finals. These early years highlighted the Royals' team dynamics, characterized by
a balanced offense led by Davies' playmaking and Cervi's intensity, paired with a gritty, teamoriented approach under Harrison's coaching that emphasized fast breaks and defensive
pressure in an era of low-scoring games. By 1948, the Royals acquired center Arnie Risen from
the Indianapolis Kautskys, adding a 6-foot-9 rebounding force who anchored the frontcourt
and bolstered their interior presence.
The 1949 merger of the NBL and Basketball Association of America (BAA) formed the National
Basketball Association (NBA), with the Royals transitioning seamlessly as one of the league's
original members. In the 1949–50 season, they reached the division finals but lost to the
Anderson Packers. The pinnacle came in 1950–51, when the Royals, again coached by
Harrison, finished second in the Western Division at 41–27 and advanced through the playoffs
by defeating the Fort Wayne Pistons 2–1 and the Minneapolis Lakers 3–1. In the NBA Finals,
they overcame the New York Knicks in a thrilling seven-game series, clinching the
championship 79–75 in Game 7 at Rochester's Edgerton Park Arena on April 21, 1951. Risen
dominated the postseason, leading all players with 273 points and 196 rebounds, earning
recognition as the unofficial Finals MVP for his 21.7 points and 14.3 rebounds per game
average. Key contributors included Davies, Bobby Wanzer, and Holzman, whose collective

guard play and Risen's post dominance exemplified the Royals' cohesive, high-impact style
that thrived on home-court energy and veteran leadership.
Throughout the early 1950s, the Royals maintained contention with additional division titles,
including a Western Division crown in 1954–55 after moving to the larger Rochester War
Memorial arena. However, persistent financial challenges in the small-market city, including
difficulties covering rising travel and operational costs amid growing league expansion,
prompted relocation discussions by the mid-1950s. These pressures, compounded by
attendance fluctuations despite the 1951 title, ultimately led the Harrisons to sell and relocate
the franchise to Cincinnati in 1957, where it became the Cincinnati Royals.

Cincinnati Royals era (1957–1972)
The Rochester Royals franchise relocated to Cincinnati in April 1957 amid financial struggles
and sagging attendance in their previous home market, where suburban migration and
economic pressures had eroded fan support. Owned by brothers Lester and Jack Harrison, the
team rebranded as the Cincinnati Royals and began play at the Cincinnati Gardens, carrying
over a competitive roster that included All-Star forward Maurice Stokes, the 1956 Rookie of
the Year. The move to a larger Midwestern city was intended to stabilize the franchise, but
initial results were mixed.
The Royals posted a respectable 33-39 record in their inaugural 1957-58 season, finishing
third in the NBA's Western Division, but attendance and performance soon faltered. They
endured back-to-back dismal campaigns of 19-53 in 1958-59 and 19-56 in 1959-60,
hampered by injuries—including Stokes' tragic career-ending collapse from a head injury—and
coaching instability under Bobby Wanzer and Tom Marshall. These struggles reflected broader
challenges in adapting to the new market, with the team drawing modest crowds despite the
city's basketball enthusiasm. A turning point came in 1960 with the territorial draft selection of
University of Cincinnati star Oscar Robertson as the first overall pick, injecting star power and
local appeal into the franchise.
Robertson's arrival sparked a remarkable resurgence in the 1960s, transforming the Royals into
perennial contenders. As a rookie in 1960-61, he averaged 30.5 points, 10.1 rebounds, and 9.7
assists per game while earning All-Star honors and Rookie of the Year accolades, though the
team managed only a 33-46 mark. Over his decade with Cincinnati (1960-1970), Robertson
became the league's premier guard, averaging a triple-double across his first five seasons—
peaking at 30.8 points, 12.5 rebounds, and 11.4 assists in 1961-62, the first such feat in NBA
history. He earned 10 All-Star selections, nine All-NBA First Team nods, and the 1964 MVP
award after leading the league with 11.0 assists per game and posting 31.4 points and 9.5
rebounds. Under coaches like Jack McMahon, the Royals achieved their best results in 196364, compiling a 55-25 record and finishing second in the Eastern Division before defeating

the Philadelphia 76ers in the semifinals but falling 4-3 to the Boston Celtics in the division
finals.
Complementing Robertson were fellow Hall of Famers Jerry Lucas and Wayne Embry, who
anchored the frontcourt during the team's peak years. Lucas, selected via territorial pick in
1962 and debuting in 1963-64, won Rookie of the Year that season with 17.0 points and 17.4
rebounds per game, earning six straight All-Star berths and three All-NBA First Team honors
through 1969 while renowned for his rebounding prowess. Embry, drafted 12th overall in 1958,
provided rugged interior play as a five-time All-Star (1961-1965) and helped stabilize the
lineup during the early Robertson era before being traded in 1966. Bolstered by these stars
and role players like Adrian Smith, the Royals posted winning records from 1961-62 (43-37)
through 1965-66 (45-35), consistently qualifying for the playoffs but unable to advance past
the Eastern Division semifinals or finals amid dominance by the Celtics and 76ers.
The late 1960s brought decline as injuries, trades, and roster turnover eroded the core.
Robertson remained elite, but the team slipped to 39-43 in 1967-68 and 39-42 in 1968-69,
missing the playoffs for the first time since 1960. Ownership instability exacerbated issues:
after initial control by the Harrisons, the franchise passed to Thomas E. Wood in 1958, then
Louis Jacobs in 1963, and finally Jacobs' sons Max and Jeremy in 1968 amid a group purchase
by Cincinnati Ventures. Attendance lagged below 5,000 per game by 1971, strained by
competition from college basketball and economic factors, leading the owners to explore
relocation options. In March 1972, the Royals were sold for $1.5 million to a Kansas CityOmaha syndicate, prompting the franchise's move after the 30-52 season, ending 15 years in
Cincinnati without a division title or Finals appearance.

Kansas City–Omaha/Kansas City Kings era (1972–1985)
In 1972, the franchise relocated from Cincinnati to the Midwest, becoming the Kansas City–
Omaha Kings after the name "Royals" was avoided to prevent confusion with the local Major
League Baseball team of the same name. The move involved splitting home games between
Kansas City's Municipal Auditorium and Omaha's Civic Auditorium, reflecting a dual-market
strategy to boost viability in the region. However, attendance in Omaha proved insufficient,
leading the team to drop the city from its name in 1975 and consolidate operations in Kansas
City at the newly opened Kemper Arena, though occasional games continued in Omaha until
1978.
The early years in Kansas City–Omaha were marked by the impact of the 1970 trade of
franchise cornerstone Oscar Robertson to the Milwaukee Bucks for guards Flynn Robinson and
Charlie Paulk, which had left the team without its dominant leader and contributed to ongoing
roster instability even after the relocation. Acquired prior to the move, Nate Archibald
emerged as a star, leading the NBA in scoring with 34.0 points per game and assists with 11.4
per game during the 1972–73 season, earning All-Star honors and helping the Kings to a 36–

46 record. Later additions like forward Scott Wedman, obtained in a 1974 trade from the
Boston Celtics, and guard Otis Birdsong, acquired from the Phoenix Suns in 1977, bolstered
the lineup, with Birdsong earning three consecutive All-Star selections from 1979 to 1981.
The franchise experienced its most successful stretch in the late 1970s and early 1980s,
culminating in a franchise-best 48–34 record during the 1978–79 season under coach Cotton
Fitzsimmons, which secured the Midwest Division title and a playoff berth. The following
year, the Kings posted a 47–35 mark and advanced in the postseason, though they fell in the
first round to the Phoenix Suns. In 1980–81, despite a 40–42 regular season, the team
reached the Western Conference Finals, where they were defeated by the [Los Angeles
Lakers](/page/Los Angeles_Lakers) in five games, highlighting the contributions of
Birdsong and Wedman in the playoffs. Forward Reggie King, drafted 18th overall in 1979,
provided consistent scoring and rebounding through his tenure until 1983, adding depth to
the frontcourt.
Ownership changes plagued the era, beginning with a 1973 sale to a group of ten Kansas City
businessmen for over $5 million, followed by a 1982 restructuring involving three primary
partners amid financial strains. In June 1983, the team was sold for $10.5 million to a new
ownership group led by figures including Frank Tyrell and Sacramento interests, signaling
instability as the buyers eyed relocation options. Kemper Arena's limitations, including
shared use with the NHL's Kansas City Scouts (later Colorado Rockies), aging facilities, and
chronically low attendance—averaging under 10,000 fans per game in later years—
exacerbated these issues, hindering revenue and fan engagement. By 1985, with the Kings
at 31–51 and facing ongoing economic challenges, the ownership announced the relocation to
Sacramento on May 15, approved by the NBA Board of Governors that June, ending the
Midwest chapter for the 1985–86 season.

Early Sacramento Kings era (1985–1998)
The Sacramento Kings relocated from Kansas City to Sacramento in 1985, following the
expiration of their lease at Kemper Arena and an NBA approval of the move on April 4, 1985.
The team's early rosters featured veterans like Reggie Theus and LaSalle Thompson, who
anchored the lineup alongside players such as Eddie Johnson and Mike Woodson. In their
inaugural 1985–86 season, the Kings finished with a 37–45 record and made the playoffs, but
subsequent years were marked by consistent losing, including a franchise-worst 17–65 mark in
1988–89.
The Kings attempted to rebuild through the draft in the late 1980s and early 1990s, selecting
shooting guard Ricky Berry seventh overall in 1988, who averaged 10.6 points per game as a
rookie before being traded after three seasons. In 1989, they acquired center Pervis Ellison,
the first overall pick, via trade from the Washington Bullets, but his tenure lasted only 34
games before he was dealt to the same team in 1990. Forward Lionel Simmons, selected

seventh overall in 1990 out of La Salle University, provided scoring with a rookie average of
20.4 points but departed via trade after five seasons in 1995.
A pivotal shift occurred in November 1991 when the Kings traded Billy Owens, the 1991 AllRookie First Team forward, to the Golden State Warriors for guard Mitch Richmond.
Richmond, who became the team's cornerstone through 1998, earned four NBA All-Star
selections from 1993 to 1996 and averaged 23.4 points per game during his Sacramento
tenure. Under his leadership, the Kings posted their best records of the era, including 25 wins
in 1990–91 and 27 wins in 1997–98, though they remained outside the playoffs. Richmond
was later inducted into the Naismith Memorial Basketball Hall of Fame in 2014.
Coaching instability contributed to the team's struggles, with Dick Motta leading from 1988 to
1990 before being fired amid a 44–116 record over his full seasons. Interim stints followed,
including Rex Hughes in 1991–92, until Garry St. Jean took over in 1992 and coached through
1997, compiling a 159–236 mark while overseeing Richmond's prime years.

The Greatest Show on Court (1998–2006)
The arrival of Chris Webber marked a turning point for the Sacramento Kings, as the team
traded guard Mitch Richmond and forward Otis Thorpe to the Washington Wizards for the AllStar forward on May 14, 1998. This acquisition complemented an emerging core that
included forward Peja Stojaković, drafted in 1996 and emerging as a sharpshooting specialist,
center Vlade Divac, who signed as a free agent that summer after being bought out by the Los
Angeles Lakers, and later point guard Mike Bibby, acquired in a 2001 trade with the Vancouver
Grizzlies for Jason Williams and others. Under new head coach Rick Adelman, hired in
September 1998 following his tenure with the Golden State Warriors, the Kings adopted a
high-octane, fast-paced offensive system emphasizing ball movement, transition play, and
unselfish passing, which transformed the franchise from perennial also-rans into playoff
contenders.
The Kings' style earned them the moniker "The Greatest Show on Court," a phrase coined by
Sports Illustrated in a February 2001 cover story highlighting their entertaining, fluid basketball
that averaged a league-high 101.7 points per game during the 2000–01 season, en route to a
55–27 record and a first-round playoff upset over the Phoenix Suns. Adelman's motion
offense, often featuring Webber as a playmaking hub at power forward and Divac facilitating
from the high post, produced highlight-reel assists and drew comparisons to showtime eras of
the past, with the team ranking among the NBA's top scorers annually through the mid-2000s.
In 2001–02, Sacramento achieved franchise-best marks with a 61–21 regular-season record
and the Western Conference's No. 1 seed, defeating the Portland Trail Blazers and Dallas
Mavericks before falling to the Los Angeles Lakers in a contentious seven-game Western
Conference Finals.

The 2002–03 season saw the Kings post 59 wins and secure the Pacific Division title, but their
playoff run ended in the Western Conference Semifinals with a 4–3 loss to the Dallas
Mavericks, hampered by Webber's lingering knee issues from a prior injury. Stojaković, a key
perimeter threat, led the NBA in three-point field goals made that year with 240. The following
campaign, 2003–04, brought a 55–27 record and another Conference Semifinals appearance,
though Dallas again prevailed in seven games after Webber suffered a season-ending knee
injury in the second round. By 2004–05, the Kings managed 50 wins despite internal
challenges, only to exit in the first round against the Seattle SuperSonics, as the core began to
fray with Divac's retirement in 2004 and Stojaković's trade to the Indiana Pacers in January
2006.
The dynasty's decline accelerated in 2005–06, when Webber, who had been traded to the
Philadelphia 76ers the previous offseason amid ongoing injury concerns including a severe
knee issue from the 2003 playoffs, was no longer part of the roster; the Kings finished 44–38
and lost in the first round to the San Antonio Spurs. Adelman's tenure concluded after that
season with a 395–229 regular-season record over eight years, the most wins by any Kings
coach, but free agency losses and key departures like Stojaković eroded the team's contention
window, signaling the end of Sacramento's most successful era.

Rebuilding and transition (2006–2017)
The 2006–07 NBA season initiated a challenging rebuilding phase for the Sacramento Kings,
as the team finished with a 33–49 record and missed the playoffs, beginning a 16-season
postseason drought that stands as the longest in league history. This period was marked by
significant roster moves to shed contracts and acquire assets, including the February 16,
2008, trade of point guard Mike Bibby to the Atlanta Hawks in exchange for Anthony Johnson,
Shelden Williams, Tyronn Lue, and Lorenzen Wright. A year later, on February 18, 2009, the
Kings traded forward John Salmons and center Brad Miller to the Chicago Bulls in a three-team
deal involving the Portland Trail Blazers, receiving Andrés Nocioni, Drew Gooden, and the draft
rights to Sergio Rodríguez in return.
The Kings sought to build around young talent through the draft, selecting guard Tyreke Evans
with the fourth overall pick in 2009, who went on to win NBA Rookie of the Year honors in the
2009–10 season after averaging 20.1 points, 5.3 rebounds, and 5.8 assists per game. In
2010, they chose center DeMarcus Cousins fifth overall, a dominant interior presence who
earned All-Star selections in 2015, 2016, and 2017 during his tenure with Sacramento. The
2010–11 season offered a brief glimpse of potential with the young core, as the Kings started
9–7 before injuries and inconsistency led to a 24–58 finish, though their early performance
fueled optimism for contention.
Ownership transitioned in 2013 when a group led by Vivek Ranadivé, chairman of TIBCO
Software, purchased the franchise from the Maloof family for a then-record $534 million on

May 31, completing the sale after thwarting a potential relocation to Seattle. This stability
helped resolve arena uncertainties, culminating in the commitment to construct the Golden 1
Center, a downtown Sacramento venue that broke ground in 2014 and opened in 2016 as the
team's new home.
The era featured frequent coaching changes amid ongoing struggles, with Reggie Theus
serving from 2007 to 2009 (50–99 record), followed by Paul Westphal from 2009 to 2012
(51–99), Tyrone Corbin as interim in 2015 (7–21), and George Karl from 2015 to 2016 (33–49).

traded Cousins to the New Orleans Pelicans on February 20, 2017, for Buddy Hield, Tyreke
Evans, Langston Galloway, and draft picks, signaling another reset.

Post-Cousins rebuild (2017–2022)
The Sacramento Kings traded star center DeMarcus Cousins and forward Omri Casspi to the
New Orleans Pelicans on February 20, 2017, in exchange for guard Buddy Hield, Tyreke Evans,
Langston Galloway, and a protected 2017 first-round draft pick, which the Kings used to select
Frank Mason III at No. 13 overall. This move marked a pivotal shift in the franchise's
direction, ending the Cousins era amid ongoing playoff droughts and front-office instability,
while acquiring young talent to build around. Hield quickly emerged as a key asset, developing
into one of the NBA's premier sharpshooters with his consistent long-range accuracy.
In the 2017 NBA Draft, the Kings selected point guard De'Aaron Fox with the fifth overall pick,
pairing him with the defensive-minded backcourt acquired in the Cousins trade. The
following year, they chose power forward Marvin Bagley III second overall in the 2018 NBA
Draft, aiming to bolster the frontcourt with athletic scoring. Under head coach Dave Joerger,
the Kings showed signs of progress in the 2018–19 season, finishing with a 39–43 record—
their most wins since 2006 and enough to keep playoff hopes alive until the final day, though
they missed out via tiebreaker. Fox's development was central to this improvement, as he
evolved from a rookie averaging 10.6 points per game to a sophomore breakout with 17.3
points, 7.3 assists, and elite speed driving the offense. Despite the momentum, Joerger was
fired shortly after the season, citing philosophical differences with management.
The 2019–20 season brought further challenges when the NBA suspended play on March 11,
2020, due to the COVID-19 pandemic, halting the Kings at a 28–36 record amid struggles to
maintain consistency. The four-month hiatus disrupted team chemistry and rhythm,
contributing to a 31–41 finish after resuming in the Orlando bubble, where the Kings played
exhibition and seeding games but fell short of the play-in tournament. Fox continued his
ascent, averaging a career-high 21.1 points and 6.2 assists while leading the team in scoring,
solidifying his role as the franchise cornerstone. In September 2020, the Kings hired Monte
McNair as general manager and vice president of basketball operations, tasking him with
overseeing player personnel, drafts, and trades to accelerate the rebuild.

The 2020–21 and 2021–22 seasons saw modest records of 31–41 and 30–52, respectively,
but Fox's growth provided optimism, as he posted 25.2 points, 6.3 assists, and improved
efficiency in 2021–22, hinting at All-Star caliber play. Bagley contributed scoring but was
hampered by injuries, while Hield remained a reliable perimeter threat. Amid ongoing
underperformance, the Kings fired head coach Luke Walton in November 2021 after a 6–11
start to the 2021–22 season, promoting Alvin Gentry to interim coach. Seeking a fresh
approach, the franchise hired Mike Brown as head coach in May 2022, bringing his experience
from multiple NBA teams and a championship as Steve Kerr's assistant in Golden State to
foster defensive discipline and player development. Under McNair's leadership, these
foundational pieces—particularly Fox's maturation—laid the groundwork for future contention.

Fox and Sabonis era (2022–present)
The Fox and Sabonis era began with the Sacramento Kings' return to the playoffs after a 16year absence, marking a significant resurgence under head coach Mike Brown, who had been
hired in 2022. In the 2022–23 season, the Kings achieved a franchise-record 48 wins with a
48–34 record, securing the third seed in the Western Conference for the first time since
2002. Led by point guard De'Aaron Fox and center Domantas Sabonis, the team emphasized a
high-paced offense that ranked first in the league with 120.7 points per game, ending their
playoff drought dating back to 2006. However, they fell in the first round to the sixth-seeded
Golden State Warriors in seven games, with the series decided by Stephen Curry's 50-point
performance in Game 7.
Domantas Sabonis emerged as a cornerstone of the Kings' success, finishing seventh in MVP
voting while leading the NBA with 14 triple-doubles during the regular season, averaging 19.4
points, 12.6 rebounds, and 7.3 assists. His versatility anchored the team's rebounding and
playmaking, contributing to their first 50-win potential since the early 2000s. De'Aaron Fox
complemented Sabonis with explosive scoring and defense, earning All-NBA Third Team
honors after averaging 26.6 points, 6.6 assists, and 2.2 steals per game, while also winning the
NBA Clutch Player of the Year award for his late-game heroics. These individual accolades
highlighted the duo's synergy, propelling Sacramento to a 25–16 road record, the best in the
Western Conference.
The momentum carried into the 2023–24 season, where the Kings posted a 46–36 record but
slipped to ninth in the West amid a competitive conference. They advanced in the play-in
tournament by defeating the Warriors 118–94 but were eliminated by the New Orleans Pelicans
105–98, missing the playoffs for the second straight year. Sabonis continued his dominance,
leading the league with 26 triple-doubles and earning his second consecutive All-NBA Third
Team selection, though the team's defensive inconsistencies, ranking 17th in points allowed at
114.8 per game, limited deeper success. Fox remained a scoring force with 26.6 points per
game, but injuries and fatigue impacted the backcourt.

Challenges intensified in the 2024–25 season, as the Kings started 13–18 and fired Mike
Brown on December 27, 2024, amid a five-game losing streak. Assistant coach Doug Christie
took over as interim head coach, guiding the team to a 27–24 finish for an overall 40–42
record and ninth-place standing in the West. The midseason transition stabilized the roster,
but the Kings were ousted in the play-in tournament with a 106–120 loss to the Dallas
Mavericks. A pivotal move came on February 5, 2025, when Sacramento traded Fox to the San
Antonio Spurs in a three-team deal that brought guard Zach LaVine and draft assets in return,
signaling a roster retool around Sabonis. Sabonis led the team with 19.1 points, 13.9 rebounds,
and 6.0 assists, finishing fifth in MVP voting despite the upheaval.
The 2025–26 Sacramento Kings season is the 81st season for the franchise in the National
Basketball Association (NBA), and 41st season in Sacramento. Entering the 2025–26 season,
the Kings committed to Doug Christie as permanent head coach on a multiyear contract
announced April 29, 2025, following his interim success. Roster adjustments included a fiveyear, $140 million extension for forward Keegan Murray in October 2025. Key offseason moves
shaped the team: on July 7, 2025, the Kings acquired guard Dennis Schröder and a 2029
second-round pick from the Detroit Pistons in exchange for a protected 2026 second-round
pick (Charlotte-owned). On July 13, 2025, they traded center Jonas Valančiūnas to the Denver
Nuggets for forward Dario Šarić, with both teams receiving trade exceptions. At the February 1,
2026 trade deadline, the Kings were involved in a three-team trade, acquiring forward
De’Andre Hunter from the Cleveland Cavaliers by sending guards Keon Ellis and Dennis
Schröder to Cleveland and sending Šarić and their least favorable 2029 second-round pick to
the Chicago Bulls; Chicago sent Emanuel Miller and a 2027 second-round pick (Denverowned) to Cleveland. Other roster moves included multiple 10-day contracts and a rest-ofseason signing for guard Killian Hayes in February-March 2026, plus two-way signings for
Patrick Baldwin Jr. and Daeqwon Plowden. The veteran roster featured players such as Russell
Westbrook, DeMar DeRozan, Zach LaVine, Keegan Murray, De’Andre Hunter, and Domantas
Sabonis. The team struggled significantly, posting a poor record around 18-53 as of late March
2026 and sitting near the bottom of the Western Conference. The season was marked by
ongoing injuries to stars like Domantas Sabonis (limited by a knee meniscus injury since
November 2025) and a shift in focus toward draft positioning rather than contention, with
poor rankings in offensive efficiency, defensive rating, rebounding percentage, and true

Identity
