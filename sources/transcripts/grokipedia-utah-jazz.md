The Utah Jazz are a professional basketball team competing in the National Basketball
Association (NBA) as a member of the league's Western Conference Northwest Division.
Founded in 1974 as an expansion franchise in New Orleans, Louisiana, the team was named
after the city's renowned jazz music heritage. Due to financial difficulties, including high
operational costs and poor attendance, the franchise relocated to Salt Lake City, Utah, in 1979,
where it retained the "Jazz" moniker despite the cultural disconnect. The team plays its home
games at the Delta Center, a multi-purpose arena in downtown Salt Lake City.
As of February 27, 2026, the Jazz have compiled an all-time regular-season record of 2,212
wins and 1,961 losses, achieving a .530 winning percentage, and have qualified for the playoffs
31 times. As of February 27, 2026, the Jazz have a record of 18–41 in the 2025–26 season
following a 129–118 loss to the New Orleans Pelicans on February 26, 2026, which extended
their four-game losing streak.
The Jazz are best known for the dynamic duo of Hall of Fame power forward Karl Malone and
point guard John Stockton, who played together from 1985 to 2003 and hold the NBA records
for most career assists (15,806 by Stockton) and free throws made (13,188 by Malone).
Other notable Hall of Famers associated with the franchise include Pete Maravich, Adrian
Dantley, and coach Jerry Sloan. Since 2022, the team has been coached by Will Hardy, and
it is currently owned by tech entrepreneur Ryan Smith, with Justin Zanik serving as general
manager. The Jazz's G League affiliate is the Salt Lake City Stars.

History
New Orleans origins (1974–1979)
The New Orleans Jazz franchise was established as an expansion team in the National
Basketball Association (NBA) on June 7, 1974, when a nine-member investment group
primarily composed of Californians paid $6.15 million for the league's 18th franchise. Led by
restaurateur Sam Battistone, whose Sambo's restaurant chain provided much of the funding,
the group selected the name "Jazz" to honor New Orleans' rich musical heritage as the
birthplace of jazz, a genre that originated in the city's diverse cultural scene in the late 19th
and early
20th centuries. The team began play in the 1974–75 season at the Louisiana

Superdome, marking the NBA's first expansion since 1968 and occurring just before the 1976
ABA-NBA merger that added four more teams to the league.
To build around a star attraction, the Jazz acquired guard Pete Maravich from the Atlanta
Hawks in a May 1974 trade that sent the team's first- and second-round draft picks from 1974
and 1975 to Atlanta in exchange for the former LSU standout, who had been the No. 3 overall
pick in 1970. Known as "Pistol Pete" for his flashy ball-handling and no-look passing,
Maravich became the franchise's cornerstone, averaging 25.6 points per game over his five
seasons with the Jazz while earning three All-Star selections and two All-NBA First Team
honors. His high-scoring performances, including a league-leading 31.1 points per game in
1976–77, drew crowds and provided highlights amid the team's struggles, though persistent
knee injuries limited him to 313 games during this period; Maravich's showmanship later
influenced future Jazz guards like John Stockton in emphasizing creativity on the court.
The Jazz posted mediocre results from the outset, compiling an overall record of 161 wins and
249 losses across five seasons without a single playoff appearance, as they finished no higher
than fourth in the Central Division. The inaugural 1974–75 campaign ended at 23–59 under
multiple coaches, including an initial stint by Scotty Robertson, a brief interim by Elgin Baylor,
and the bulk by Butch van Breda Kolff. Subsequent years saw modest improvements,
peaking at 39–43 in 1977–78 under Baylor, who took over as head coach in 1976 and guided
the team through its final three New Orleans seasons, but the franchise remained mired in
mediocrity.
Supporting Maravich were a mix of role players and mid-tier talents, including forwards Lenny
Randle, who averaged 12.8 points in 1976–77 before a controversial off-court incident led to
his trade, and Truck Robinson, a 1978 All-Star who joined in 1978–79 and led the team with
21.5 points and 10.7 rebounds per game in his debut season with New Orleans. Other
contributors like center Joe Meriweather and guard Jim McElroy provided depth, but the roster
lacked the balance needed for contention, often relying on Maravich's scoring to stay
competitive.
Financial difficulties plagued the franchise almost immediately, exacerbated by low
attendance after an initial novelty boost, inadequate arena facilities at the aging Municipal
Auditorium before moving to the Superdome, and the annual disruption of playing up to a
month of home games on the road during Mardi Gras in February. By the late 1970s,
mounting losses—estimated at over $10 million cumulatively—prompted ownership to explore
relocation options, as the team averaged 8,883 fans per game in its final season and faced
ongoing operational challenges in a market better suited to college and other pro sports.

Relocation and early Utah years (1979–1984)

The Utah Jazz franchise relocated from New Orleans to Salt Lake City in June 1979, prompted
by ongoing financial struggles including low attendance and mounting debts in its original
market. Owner Sam Battistone, who had acquired the team in 1974, spearheaded the move
to Utah as a means of stabilizing operations in a smaller but enthusiastic market. The
relocation was facilitated by local business leaders and community support, including
assurances from Latter-day Saint (LDS) Church officials that the team would receive backing
to succeed in the predominantly Mormon state.
In their inaugural Utah season of 1979–80, the Jazz finished with a 24–58 record under coach
Tom Nissalke, adapting to the challenges of a smaller media market and cultural differences,
such as the conservative values and family-oriented environment influenced by the state's
large LDS population. The team played home games at the Salt Palace, a 12,666-seat arena,
where average attendance reached 7,821 fans per game, reflecting initial curiosity but also
hurdles in building a loyal base in a region unaccustomed to professional basketball. Efforts
to foster community ties included player appearances at local events and school programs,
with the franchise emphasizing family-friendly initiatives to align with Utah's demographic and
cultural context.
Frank Layden (1927–2025), hired as general manager in 1979 shortly before the move, played
a key role in the transition by overseeing operations and later assuming head coaching duties
in December 1981 after Nissalke's dismissal; he died on July 9, 2025, at age 93. Under
Layden's leadership from December 1981 to December 1988, the Jazz compiled a 277–255
regular-season record as he prioritized defensive fundamentals and player development to
instill discipline in a young roster. The team missed the playoffs in each of the early years
before 1984, finishing no higher than fifth in the Midwest Division, but Layden's tenure laid
groundwork for future success by drafting foundational talents like John Stockton in 1984.
Notable additions bolstered the roster during this period, including forward Adrian Dantley,
acquired via trade from the Los Angeles Lakers in September 1979 in exchange for Spencer
Haywood, who became a scoring mainstay averaging over 24 points per game in his first two
seasons with Utah. The 1980 NBA Draft brought guard Darrell Griffith with the second
overall pick out of the University of Louisville; he earned Rookie of the Year honors in the
1980–81 season by averaging 20.6 points and providing explosive athleticism that energized
the team. These players formed the core of an offense-oriented squad, though defensive
inconsistencies persisted amid the rebuilding efforts.
Attendance at the Salt Palace gradually increased from the initial 7,821 average in 1979–80 to
over 10,000 per game by the 1983–84 season, signaling growing fan engagement as the Jazz
hosted community outreach like youth clinics and charity drives to cultivate loyalty in the local
market. Despite the lack of postseason appearances until 1984, these initiatives, supported
by endorsements from Utah's business and religious communities, helped embed the
franchise in the state's identity and ensured its survival during a precarious early phase.

Stockton-Malone dynasty (1984–2003)


The Utah Jazz entered their most dominant period with the arrival of point guard John
Stockton, selected 16th overall in the 1984 NBA Draft out of Gonzaga University. The
following year, power forward Karl Malone joined the franchise as the 13th overall pick from
Louisiana Tech University. Stockton and Malone formed one of the NBA's most prolific duos,
playing together for 18 seasons and combining for 37 years of service exclusively with the
Jazz, during which they amassed over 61,000 points and set benchmarks for longevity and
productivity. Their partnership emphasized unselfish play, high basketball IQ, and relentless
execution, transforming the Jazz into perennial contenders.
In December 1988, Jerry Sloan assumed the role of head coach, succeeding Frank Layden
midway through the 1988–89 season. Sloan, who retired in 2011 with a Jazz regular-season
record of 1,127–682, instilled a gritty, fundamentals-driven system that highlighted the
Stockton-Malone pick-and-roll as its cornerstone, enabling efficient scoring through screens,
reads, and finishes at the rim. This offense, paired with tenacious defense, fostered a
team culture of toughness and resilience, often characterized by physical play and minimal
turnovers, which became synonymous with the franchise's identity.
The Stockton-Malone era's early contention phase from 1988 to 1996 featured consistent
excellence, with the Jazz achieving 50 or more wins in eight consecutive seasons, including a
franchise-record 60 victories in 1994–95. Supporting cast members like sharpshooting guard
Jeff Hornacek, acquired in 1994, and defensive forward Bryon Russell provided spacing,
perimeter defense, and versatility, complementing the stars' interior dominance. The 1988
playoffs marked a pivotal milestone, as the Jazz won their first playoff series since relocating to
Utah, defeating the Seattle SuperSonics in the first round before falling to the Los Angeles
Lakers.
The dynasty peaked from 1996 to 1998, culminating in back-to-back NBA Finals appearances.
In 1996–97, the Jazz posted a league-best 64 wins and advanced through the Western
Conference, only to lose to the Chicago Bulls 4–2 in the Finals. They returned to the Finals in
1997–98 with 54 wins, again defeating strong Western foes but falling to the Bulls 4–2, denied
a championship by Michael Jordan's iconic game-winner in Game 6. These runs highlighted
the duo's prime, with Malone earning MVP honors in 1997 and both players earning All-NBA
First Team selections multiple times.
Malone garnered 14 All-Star selections during his Jazz tenure, tying for the second-most in
NBA history at the time, while Stockton established career records for assists (15,806) and
steals (3,265), many set in Utah's high-altitude confines. The late years from 1998 to 2003
saw sustained competitiveness despite mounting injuries—Malone battled chronic knee
issues, and age began to affect the core—but the Jazz still notched multiple 50-win seasons
and playoff berths until Stockton's retirement in 2003 and Malone's departure that offseason.

Post-dynasty transition (2003–2010)
Following the retirements of longtime stars John Stockton and Karl Malone after the 2002–03
season, the Utah Jazz faced a significant transition, entering an era without their iconic pickand-roll duo that had defined the franchise for nearly two decades. Stockton, the NBA's alltime leader in assists and steals, announced his retirement on May 2, 2003, at age 40, while
Malone, a two-time MVP, followed suit shortly after, opting to join the Los Angeles Lakers for
one final season before retiring in 2004. The immediate aftermath saw a dip in performance,
with the Jazz finishing the 2003–04 season at 42–40, missing the playoffs for the first time in
20 years despite Jerry Sloan's continued leadership as head coach, a tenure that began
in 1988 and emphasized disciplined, defense-oriented basketball. Sloan's steady hand
provided continuity amid the roster overhaul, as the team pivoted to younger talent to
rebuild competitiveness.
To bolster the frontcourt, the Jazz targeted free agency in 2004, signing forward Carlos Boozer
to a six-year, $68 million contract after he became a restricted free agent from the Cleveland
Cavaliers. Boozer, who had averaged 15.5 points and 10.1 rebounds in his final Cavaliers season,
brought scoring and rebounding prowess to complement the existing core. The following year,
the Jazz drafted point guard Deron Williams third overall in the 2005 NBA Draft out of the
University of Illinois, where he had led his team to the national championship game; Williams
quickly emerged as a floor general, earning All-Rookie First Team honors in his debut season
with averages of 10.8 points and 5.4 assists. These additions, alongside holdovers like forward
Andrei Kirilenko, marked the formation of a new core aimed at sustaining contention in the
competitive Western Conference.
The 2006–07 season highlighted the promise of this transitioning roster, as the Jazz captured
the Northwest Division title with a 51–31 record, their best since the Stockton-Malone era.
Kirilenko reached his defensive zenith that year, earning All-Defensive First Team honors for
the third time in four seasons while averaging 11.0 points, 5.8 rebounds, and a league-leading
3.0 blocks per game, showcasing his versatility as a "five-tool defender" capable of guarding
multiple positions. Center Mehmet Okur, acquired via trade in 2004, provided elite outside
shooting for a big man, draining 2.1 three-pointers per game at 40.5% efficiency to stretch
defenses and open driving lanes for Boozer and Williams. In the playoffs, the Williams-Boozer
duo propelled Utah to the Western Conference Finals, defeating the Houston Rockets and
Golden State Warriors before falling to the San Antonio Spurs in five games. The momentum
carried into 2007–08, with a franchise-record 54 wins and another Northwest Division crown,
though they exited in the conference semifinals against the same Spurs squad.

However, the late years of the decade brought challenges, including reported tensions within
the Williams-Boozer pairing, as Williams expressed frustration over team inconsistencies and
Boozer's occasional injury absences, contributing to early playoff disappointments. The 2008–
09 season ended with a 48–34 record and a first-round sweep by the Rockets, hampered by
Boozer's knee issues that limited him to 37 games. Despite rebounding to 53–29 in 2009–10
and reaching the conference semifinals—where they lost to the Lakers in six games—the
underlying discord foreshadowed roster upheaval. Throughout this period, ownership
remained stable under Larry H. Miller, who had purchased the team in 1986 and guided it
through the post-dynasty shift with a focus on fiscal prudence and community ties; in
November 2006, the arena was renamed EnergySolutions Arena in a sponsorship deal,
reflecting the franchise's adaptability.

Rebuilding under Sloan and beyond (2010–2017)
The Utah Jazz entered a period of significant roster turnover in 2010 and 2011, marking the
beginning of a rebuild after the departures of key veterans. In July 2010, forward Carlos Boozer
left via sign-and-trade to the Chicago Bulls, providing the Jazz with a trade exception that they
used to acquire center Al Jefferson from the Minnesota Timberwolves on July 13, 2010.
Power forward Paul Millsap, already a cornerstone since being drafted in 2006, remained with
the team and formed a formidable frontcourt duo with Jefferson during the 2010-11 season.
On February 23, 2011, the Jazz traded point guard Deron Williams to the New Jersey Nets in
exchange for point guard Devin Harris, forward Derrick Favors, two future first-round picks, and
cash considerations, signaling a shift toward younger talent.
This transitional phase was compounded by internal turmoil, culminating in the resignation of
longtime head coach Jerry Sloan on February 10, 2011, following a heated argument with
Williams during a game against the Chicago Bulls. Assistant coach Tyrone Corbin was
promoted to interim head coach that day and later given the full role, compiling a 112-146
record over parts of three seasons through April 2014. Under Corbin, the Jazz focused on
integrating newcomers like Favors, who provided defensive depth and rebounding as a young
power forward acquired in the Williams deal.
The 2013 NBA Draft further bolstered the youth movement, with the Jazz selecting point guard
Trey Burke ninth overall after trading up from the 14th pick, and center Rudy Gobert 27th
overall. Forward Gordon Hayward, a 2010 draftee who had emerged as a versatile scorer
and playmaker, became a restricted free agent after extension talks stalled in October 2013;
the Jazz retained him by matching a four-year, $63 million offer sheet from the Charlotte
Hornets on July 12, 2014.
In June 2014, the Jazz hired Quin Snyder as head coach on a three-year deal, bringing a playerdevelopment emphasis from his time as an assistant with the Atlanta Hawks and earlier head
coaching stint at the University of Missouri. Snyder quickly instilled a defensive identity

anchored by Gobert, whose length and timing disrupted opponents; in his first full season
(2014-15), Gobert averaged 2.3 blocks per game and earned All-Defensive Second Team
honors, helping the Jazz rank among the league's top shot-blocking teams. This foundation
emphasized youth development, with players like Favors contributing versatile frontcourt
depth through improved rebounding and perimeter defense.
The Jazz showed steady improvement from 2015 to 2017 under Snyder's system. They
finished the 2014-15 season at 38-44, followed by a 40-42 mark in 2015-16, ranking second in
the Western Conference in defensive rating during the latter year. In 2016-17, the team
achieved a 51-31 record, securing the No. 5 seed in the West and returning to the playoffs for
the first time since 2012, with Hayward earning his first All-Star selection after averaging
21.8 points, 5.4 rebounds, and 3.6 assists. This progress highlighted the maturation of
the young core, including Gobert's expanding role as a rim protector, setting the stage for
future contention.

Gobert-Mitchell contention (2017–2022)
The Utah Jazz selected guard Donovan Mitchell with the 13th overall pick in the 2017 NBA
draft, acquired from the Denver Nuggets in a trade involving Trey Lyles. Mitchell quickly
emerged as a key contributor, averaging 20.5 points per game as a rookie and finishing as the
runner-up for NBA Rookie of the Year behind Ben Simmons. His scoring prowess and
playmaking helped elevate the Jazz's backcourt alongside Rudy Gobert's elite rim protection,
marking the beginning of a competitive era under head coach Quin Snyder.
From the 2018-19 to 2019-20 seasons, the Jazz achieved consistent success, posting 50 wins
in 2018-19 and a league-best .722 win percentage in the shortened 2019-20 campaign
despite only 44 victories due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Gobert anchored one of the
NBA's top defenses, earning Defensive Player of the Year honors in both 2018 and 2019 for his
league-leading blocks and rebounding. The 2019-20 playoffs in the Orlando bubble were
disrupted by the pandemic, with Gobert becoming the first NBA player to test positive for
COVID-19 on March 11, 2020, leading to a league-wide suspension. The Jazz advanced to
the Western Conference Semifinals but fell to the Denver Nuggets in seven games, hampered
by injuries and the unusual environment.
In the 2020-21 season, the Jazz posted a franchise-record 52 wins and secured the No. 1 seed
in the Western Conference, propelled by an explosive offense that ranked first in the league at
117.6 points per 100 possessions. Snyder's adjustments emphasized spacing and motion
principles, incorporating more three-point volume and pick-and-roll variations to complement
Mitchell's drives and Gobert's interior presence. Key offseason moves bolstered the roster,
including the June 2019 trade for veteran point guard Mike Conley from the Memphis Grizzlies
in exchange for Jae Crowder, Kyle Korver, Grayson Allen, and draft picks, which stabilized the
backcourt. The Jazz also signed forward Bojan Bogdanović to a four-year deal in July 2019,

adding sharpshooting and versatility to the wing. Mitchell, Gobert, and Conley all earned
2021 All-Star selections—the first time since 1997 that three Jazz players were chosen—while
the team again reached the Western Conference Semifinals before losing to the Los
Angeles Clippers. Gobert claimed his third Defensive Player of the Year award that season,
underscoring the Jazz's defensive dominance with the league's second-best defensive
rating.
The 2021-22 season saw the Jazz win 49 games and claim the No. 5 Western Conference
seed, but they were eliminated in the first round by the Dallas Mavericks in six games,
exposing vulnerabilities in playoff execution. Persistent rumors of friction between Mitchell
and Gobert, stemming from the 2020 COVID incident and resurfacing through passive
comments on team performance, created internal strain that contributed to the organization's
decision to pursue roster changes. These tensions ultimately led to the trades of both stars
in the 2022 offseason, reshaping the franchise's direction toward a rebuild.

Markkanen-led rebuild (2022–present)
The Utah Jazz initiated a full rebuild in the summer of 2022 by trading away their two
cornerstone players, Rudy Gobert and Donovan Mitchell, in separate blockbuster deals that
netted a wealth of draft assets. On July 1, Gobert was sent to the Minnesota Timberwolves in
exchange for Malik Beasley, Patrick Beverley, Jarred Vanderbilt, Leandro Bolmaro, the draft
rights to Walker Kessler (No. 22 pick in the 2022 NBA Draft), and four first-round picks (2023,
2025, 2027, and 2029, with protections). Just two months later, on September 1,
Mitchell was traded to the Cleveland Cavaliers for Lauri Markkanen, Collin Sexton, Ochai
Agbaji, three unprotected first-round picks (2025, 2027, 2029), and two pick swaps (2026,
2028). These moves, orchestrated by new CEO of Basketball Operations Danny Ainge,
shifted the franchise's focus toward accumulating young talent and future draft capital to build
a sustainable contender.
Markkanen quickly emerged as the centerpiece of the rebuild, anchoring the offense with his
versatile scoring and earning All-Star honors in 2023 after averaging 25.6 points and 8.6
rebounds per game in his first season with Utah. Under Ainge's strategy, the Jazz prioritized
high draft selections through intentional tanking, amassing over a dozen first-round picks while
developing a young core to avoid long-term contention without foundational pieces. This
approach intentionally sacrificed short-term wins to maximize lottery odds and asset value,
positioning the team for a multi-year infusion of elite prospects.
The 2022–23 season marked the rebuild's early stages, with the Jazz finishing 37–45 and
missing the playoffs for the first time since 2017. Rookie center Walker Kessler provided
immediate impact on defense, averaging 9.2 points, 8.0 rebounds, and 2.4 blocks in 59
games, earning Western Conference Rookie of the Month honors in December and
establishing himself as a rim protector. Markkanen's breakout, combined with contributions

from veterans like Mike Conley (before his trade) and emerging role players, offered glimpses
of potential amid the transition.
In 2023–24, the Jazz regressed to a 31–51 record, further embracing the tanking blueprint to
secure higher draft positioning. The team selected forward Taylor Hendricks ninth overall
and guard Keyonte George 16th in the 2023 NBA Draft, adding athleticism and playmaking to
the roster; George, in particular, showed promise as a scorer, averaging 13.0 points as a rookie
and earning All-Rookie Second Team honors. These additions joined Kessler and Markkanen
in a youth movement, though the focus remained on evaluation rather than wins.
The 2024–25 campaign represented the nadir of the rebuild, as Utah posted a league-worst
17–65 record, ensuring strong lottery odds. This poor performance paid off in the 2025
NBA Draft, where the Jazz selected Rutgers forward Ace Bailey fifth overall, a high-upside
scorer projected as a top-three talent before draft-night dynamics. Bailey, known for his
explosive athleticism and scoring versatility, headlined the class alongside later picks like
guard Isaiah Collier (acquired via trade) and forward Cody Williams (from 2024). In NBA
Summer League, second-year forward Kyle Filipowski shone, averaging 29.3 points, 7.7
rebounds, and 2.3 assists over three games to earn MVP honors—the first for a Jazz player.
Entering the 2025–26 season under third-year head coach Will Hardy—who has emphasized
player development throughout the rebuild—the Jazz added veteran depth via trades,
including center Jusuf Nurkic, forward Kevin Love, guard Kyle Anderson, and forward Georges
Niang, to mentor the young core while maintaining flexibility for further asset accumulation.
With Markkanen still leading the scoring (averaging 23.2 points entering the season) and
prospects like Bailey, George, Hendricks, and Kessler gaining experience, Ainge's patient,
draft-centric approach continues to shape a roster blending immediate evaluation with longterm contention potential. The team unveiled a uniform rebrand in 2022, introducing a
black-and-yellow scheme, but reverted to its traditional purple, sky blue, and black colors in
2024 following fan feedback.
